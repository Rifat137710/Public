\begin{abstract}
Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) operation is often proposed as a voltage support resource on weak
distribution feeders. Recent studies compare learned controllers against local droop
control, but they do not establish how much support the connected fleet can actually
provide. This paper computes an upper bound on that support. For every hour of a daily
load profile, the active and reactive injection of each hub is optimized directly on the
AC power flow, limited only by inverter rating. The bound is then used to separate two
effects: the shortfall caused by limited stored energy, and the shortfall caused by the
controller. On the IEEE 34-bus feeder, coordinated dispatch across five hubs clears every
violating hour at a mild peak, while a single hub of the same rating clears fewer than
half. Under realistic availability and state-of-charge limits, however, the fleet supplies
less than 15 percent of the energy the controller requests. Energy adequacy outweighs
controller choice by more than a factor of 40 at both load levels. Training the policy
five times longer does not change this, and its commanded setpoints show why: it scales a
nearly fixed power factor instead of allocating between active and reactive power as
loading changes. A degradation-aware objective then removes about 60 percent of battery
throughput for roughly a 1 percent rise in violation, because the marginal stored
kilowatt-hour is nearly worthless once the fleet binds. Fleet
sizing and availability therefore deserve more attention than controller sophistication.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Long rural distribution feeders were not built for the voltage profiles they must now
hold. Peak demand pulls the far end of the circuit below acceptable limits, and the usual
remedies, such as reconductoring and additional regulators, are slow and expensive.
Bidirectional inverters installed with electric vehicle (EV) fleets offer another option.
They sit near the load end of the feeder, where support is most effective, and their cost
is borne by the fleet owner. Recent work applies reinforcement learning (RL) to coordinate
several V2G hubs for voltage regulation on the IEEE 34-bus feeder \cite{wang2026v2g}.

A wider body of work applies RL to inverter dispatch for the same purpose. Recent
contributions include graph-based architectures with explicit safety layers
\cite{yan2024magrl}, active management of EV charging to hold voltage in low-voltage
networks \cite{nazir2024}, battery-to-grid injection from charging and swapping stations
\cite{guldorum2024}, impact analysis of optimal bidirectional charging under
spatial-temporal constraints \cite{lee2025}, and coordination of local EV flexibility with
social impact considered alongside network limits \cite{chen2025}. EV stock continues to
grow \cite{iea2025}, and with it the capacity notionally available for such services. These
build on earlier RL formulations for volt/var control, both safe off-policy
\cite{wang2020safe} and multi-agent consensus \cite{gao2021consensus}. Across this
literature the controller is treated as the binding constraint, so that a better policy is
expected to extract more support from the same hardware.

That assumption has not been tested. None of these studies computes what the fleet could
deliver under an ideal controller, so a reported improvement cannot be placed on a scale. A
policy that halves the violation of a droop baseline may have captured most of the
available benefit, or very little of it, and the published numbers do not separate the two
cases.

This paper computes the missing reference point. For each hour we solve for the per-hub
active and reactive injection that minimizes a two-sided voltage violation metric, using
the AC power flow and limiting only inverter rating. No energy constraint is applied, so
the result is an upper bound set by the power electronics alone. Comparing it against droop
and learned controllers, run with and without a realistic fleet model, splits the total
shortfall into an energy part and a control part.

Two design choices make that split trustworthy. Voltages are evaluated per phase rather
than as bus averages, because the standard that defines the limits is itself per phase and
a three-phase average hides single-phase excursions on an unbalanced feeder. Controllers
are then compared on common random numbers, so every controller sees the same availability
draw, the same initial state of charge and the same load realization, and the differences
between them are paired rather than compared as independent means. Without pairing, the
controller gap reported here would sit inside the scenario-to-scenario noise.

The practical stake is where a utility or fleet operator should spend effort. If the
controller binds, the return comes from better control software, which is cheap to deploy
across an existing installation. If stored energy binds, the return comes from more
vehicles, longer dwell times or contractual availability, none of which a control upgrade
can supply. These lead to different investments, and the two cases are distinguishable
before any controller is built.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We compute an hourly upper bound on multi-hub V2G voltage support for the IEEE 34-bus
feeder and use it to split the shortfall into energy and control parts.
\item We measure the active and reactive trade-off per unit of stored energy rather than
per unit of apparent power.
\item We compare droop and learned control under realistic availability and
state-of-charge limits, and sweep the training budget to five times its usual value.
\item We report a degradation-aware dispatch frontier and three negative results.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the
feeder, the fleet model, the metrics and the controllers. Section~\ref{sec:ceiling} computes
the upper bound and splits the shortfall. Section~\ref{sec:controllers} reports controller
performance, Section~\ref{sec:degradation} the degradation frontier,
Section~\ref{sec:nulls} three further observations, and Section~\ref{sec:conc} concludes.

\subsection{Fleet and Battery Model}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig1_decomposition.pdf}}
\caption{Day-integrated voltage violation on the five-hub feeder.}
\label{fig:decomp}
\end{figure*}

Each hub serves a fleet whose availability varies through the day between 45 and 85 percent
and is drawn independently per scenario. Vehicles arrive at 70 percent state of charge and
are not discharged below 20 percent. State of charge is drawn down by apparent power, so
reactive support costs the same stored energy as active support. This follows the reference
formulation and matters for Section~\ref{sec:pq}.

The distinction between an unconstrained and a constrained fleet runs through the whole
study. The reference work evaluates multi-hub coordination while ``assuming sufficient EV
capacity at each hub'' \cite{wang2026v2g}. We report both cases, because the difference
between them turns out to be the principal result.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

ANSI C84.1 \cite{ansi} is a per-phase standard, so voltages are evaluated per phase rather
than as bus averages, which mask single-phase excursions on this unbalanced feeder. The
primary metric is the day-integrated two-sided violation
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IV} = \sum_{h}\sum_{p} \Big[ \max(0, v_{\min} - v_{p,h}) + \max(0, v_{p,h} - v_{\max}) \Big] \Delta t
\label{eq:iv}
\end{equation}
in per-unit-hours, with $v_{\min}=0.95$, $v_{\max}=1.05$ and $\Delta t = 1$~h. Unlike a
count of violation hours, \eqref{eq:iv} does not saturate when two controllers violate in
the same hours by different margins, and being two-sided it penalizes overvoltage as well.

All comparisons use common random numbers. Every controller sees the same availability
draw, initial state of charge and load realization, and differences are paired. Confidence
intervals are 95 percent over the paired scenarios and training seeds.

\subsection{Control Algorithms}

The droop baseline is a Volt-Watt and Volt-VAr characteristic with a $\pm 0.02$~p.u.
deadband, consistent with IEEE~1547 \cite{ieee1547}. Droop response and feeder voltage are
mutually dependent, so the baseline is not evaluated open-loop. We obtain the fixed point by
damped Jacobi updates with a residual convergence test, a detail examined in
Section~\ref{sec:nulls}.

The learned controller is soft actor-critic \cite{haarnoja2018sac} as implemented in
Stable-Baselines3 \cite{raffin2021sb3}, with two hidden layers of 256 units, a learning rate
of $3\times10^{-4}$ and a batch size of 256. Episodes span one day, so that rationing of a
finite battery over time is representable. The state comprises bus voltages, load multiplier,
hour of day, and per-hub state of charge and availability. The action sets per-hub active and
reactive power as a residual on the droop prior.


\section{Voltage Support Capability}
\label{sec:ceiling}

\subsection{Upper Bound on Voltage Support}

For each hour we search for the per-hub injection $(P_i, Q_i)$ that minimizes \eqref{eq:iv}
subject to $\sqrt{P_i^2+Q_i^2} \le S_i^{\mathrm{rated}}$, by coordinate descent over a refined
polar grid with a full AC power flow at every candidate and no energy constraint imposed.
Non-converged solutions are masked rather than read, because at deep-sag hours the
high-injection end of the search lies past the nose of the power-voltage curve.

Searching per hub rather than uniformly is necessary. With five hubs at equal output, raising
the feeder end to 0.95~p.u. drives the near buses above 1.05~p.u., so hours an uneven dispatch
clears comfortably are scored unclearable, and the five-hub case then scores worse than the
single-hub case, which is not physically possible. Per-hub search removes the artefact and
moves the mild multi-hub result from five clearable hours to eighteen.

Table~\ref{tab:ceiling} reports the outcome. A single hub at full rating clears seven of the
eighteen loaded hours at the mild peak and two at the aggressive peak. Five hubs of the same
individual rating, dispatched unevenly, clear all eighteen and four respectively.
Fig.~\ref{fig:ceiling} shows the hourly picture. The reachable envelope sits on or above the
0.95~p.u. limit for the entire mild day, and falls short of it for eleven hours of the
aggressive day regardless of dispatch. Those eleven hours are a siting and rating problem, not
a control problem, and no policy can clear them. At equal total inverter rating, spreading
capacity across five hubs and dispatching it unevenly reduces violation by 100 percent at the
mild peak and by 36.5 percent at the aggressive peak, relative to uniform dispatch.

\subsection{Energy and Control Contributions}


Fig.~\ref{fig:decomp} places the controllers against this bound and is the central result of
the paper. Reading the mild panel, the untreated feeder accumulates 45.61~p.u.$\cdot$h of
violation and the bound removes all of it. A droop law with unlimited stored energy removes
41.46, or 91 percent, while the same law with a realistic fleet removes 2.84, or 6 percent.
The fleet delivers 1342~kWh against the 9264~kWh the unconstrained law requests, a ratio of
14.5 percent. At the aggressive peak the ratio is 5.7 percent.

The controller span lies inside that shortfall. Between tuned droop and the best learned
policy we obtained, the difference is 0.79~p.u.$\cdot$h at the mild peak and 2.58 at the
aggressive peak, against energy shortfalls of 38.62 and 115.69. Energy adequacy therefore
outweighs controller choice by a factor of 49 and 45.

\subsection{Active and Reactive Trade-off}
\label{sec:pq}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig2_ceiling.pdf}}
\caption{Worst-phase voltage by hour, with and without optimized five-hub dispatch.}
\label{fig:ceiling}
\end{figure}

Because state of charge is drawn down by apparent power, the common assumption that reactive
power is free does not hold. Sweeping the injection angle at fixed apparent power and fixed
loading, we find that active power delivers between 1.25 and 1.49 times the worst-phase
voltage rise of reactive power for the same battery energy withdrawn. The voltage-optimal
split is 35 to 40 degrees from the active axis at half rating, and moves towards 17 to 26
degrees at full rating as the resistive voltage drop becomes dominant. The hub rating ratio of
400~kVAr to 500~kW corresponds to 38.7 degrees.

This carries a design consequence. On a feeder with this R/X character, a hub sized by
apparent power and operated at its rating ratio commits more reactive power than is useful,
and pays full battery energy for it.

\subsection{Multi-Hub Operation}

Two features of the table are worth noting. The learned policy consumes 5842~kWh of battery
throughput at the mild peak against 1342~kWh for droop, and converts that into a worse voltage
outcome. At the mild peak its result is statistically indistinguishable from installing no V2G
at all. Both controllers respect the upper voltage limit exactly, at 1.050~p.u., a point taken
up in Section~\ref{sec:nulls}.

\subsection{Training Analysis}

A negative result about a learned controller invites the objection that it was not trained
long enough, and at 20,000 steps, about 1100 episodes for a ten-dimensional action space, the
objection is reasonable. We therefore trained to 100,000 steps and evaluated at 20,000-step
checkpoints on identical scenarios, placing checkpoints on the chunk boundaries the
fixed-budget runs use so the 20,000-step row measures the agent of
Table~\ref{tab:controllers}.

The outcome differs by load level, as Fig.~\ref{fig:learning} shows. At the aggressive peak the
curve is flat from 40,000 steps onward. Violation moves from 169.20 to 168.41 across a fivefold
increase in budget, which lies inside the combined confidence interval of 1.13, and droop wins
every paired scenario at every checkpoint. The advantage of droop under aggressive stress is
therefore not an artefact of training budget.

At the mild peak the curve is still improving at 100,000 steps. Violation falls from 45.05 to
43.56, a change of $-1.48$ against a confidence interval of 0.49. The gap to droop narrows
monotonically from $+2.27$ to $+0.79$, and the policy takes two of ten paired scenarios, the
only scenarios it wins anywhere in this study. We therefore report the mild comparison as
unresolved at the budgets tested rather than as a droop victory. Set against the previous
section, however, the gap narrowed by 1.48~p.u.$\cdot$h over a fivefold increase in training,
against an energy shortfall of 38.62 at the same operating point.

\subsection{Allocation Analysis}


A scoreboard alone does not explain the outcome, so we examined the active and reactive split
the policy commands, hour by hour, weighted by apparent power and compared against the
voltage-optimal angle recomputed at the injection level the policy itself selected.

The policy does not track the optimum, and the deviation does not fall with more training. At
20,000 steps the mean absolute deviation is 26.5 degrees at the mild peak and 32.5 degrees at
the aggressive peak. At 100,000 steps these become 20.8 and 31.5 degrees. The changes of
$-5.7$ and $-1.0$ degrees both lie inside their combined 95 percent confidence intervals of
24.2 and 35.1 degrees, so neither is a reduction the data supports.

Fig.~\ref{fig:pq} shows the hourly picture at 100,000 steps. The voltage-optimal angle stays
between 29 and 35 degrees for most of the day at both load levels. At the mild peak the
commanded angle stays near 20 degrees, with an apparent-power-weighted daily mean of 18.8
degrees. At the aggressive peak the daily mean falls to 1.2 degrees, which is almost pure
active power, and in 50 percent of sampled hub-hours the commanded reactive power is negative.
Reactive absorption lowers voltage on this feeder, so these are actions of the wrong sign for
the condition being corrected, not merely conservative ones. The pattern is consistent with a
controller that scales a roughly fixed power factor instead of allocating between active and
reactive power in response to loading.


\section{Degradation-Aware Dispatch}
\label{sec:degradation}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig5_frontier.pdf}}
\caption{Violation against battery throughput as the wear weight is swept from 0 to 300.}
\label{fig:frontier}
\end{figure}

Battery wear is the standing objection to V2G participation, and the economic case is
sensitive to it \cite{v2gaging2024}. The reference formulation carries no battery term in its
reward. We added an amp-hour throughput cost, expressed on the same scale as the voltage
penalty, and swept its weight across three orders of magnitude.

The resulting frontier is flat, as Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} shows. Across a 300-fold sweep the
violation stays within a 2.6 percent band, between 44.83 and 46.01 per-unit-hours, while daily
throughput falls from 6188 to 2449~kWh. Comparing the endpoints, roughly 60 percent of battery
throughput can be removed for a 1.1 percent increase in violation.

This follows from the energy-limited regime identified earlier. When the fleet cannot supply
what the feeder needs, the marginal voltage value of the last kilowatt-hour discharged is very
low, so a wear penalty removes it at almost no cost. On feeders in this regime, aggressive
degradation-aware dispatch therefore costs little in voltage performance, which improves the
participation case for fleet owners.

The intermediate points should not be over-interpreted: each weight was trained with a single
seed and the sequence is not monotone, so the defensible claims are the endpoint comparison and
the width of the band, not the shape in between.

\section{Additional Observations}
\label{sec:nulls}

This section reports three checks that did not produce the expected effect. Each addresses a
design choice that is common in the literature.

\subsection{Safety Projection}

Projecting commanded setpoints onto the voltage-feasible set is standard practice in
learning-based volt/var control \cite{wang2020safe,yan2024magrl}, so we implemented and
measured two: a proportional reduction along the commanded ray, and a reactive-first variant
motivated by Section~\ref{sec:pq}.

Both are inert. The unprojected policy already reports zero overvoltage and a maximum phase
voltage of exactly 1.050~p.u. at both load levels, and projection changes the violation metric
by less than the confidence interval. The feasibility test acts on commanded setpoints, but the
state-of-charge limit clips those commands first, so the energy constraint binds before the
voltage constraint and leaves nothing to remove. Where overvoltage does
appear, it tracks the training regime rather than the controller: policies trained on
independently sampled load multipliers without a fleet in the loop reach 1.229~p.u., whereas
day-structured training with the fleet in the loop holds 1.050 everywhere we tested.


\subsection{Fleet State}

Adding state of charge and availability to the observation, information the reference
formulation does not provide, does not improve voltage performance. At the mild peak the
augmented agent reaches 45.41$\,\pm\,$0.36 against 44.91$\,\pm\,$0.29 for a voltage-only
observation, an overlapping interval. What it changes is energy use. The augmented agent
spends 6068~kWh against 2497~kWh for no violation benefit, while the voltage-only agent
breaches the upper limit at the aggressive peak where the augmented agent does not.

\subsection{Droop Convergence}

The last observation is methodological and affects any study using a closed-loop droop
baseline, whose fixed point is commonly computed with a fixed iteration count. On the five-hub
feeder, at a damping factor of 0.6, the iteration does not settle but enters a period-two limit
cycle, with total active power alternating between 1298 and 630~kW at the mild-peak evening
hour. A fixed iteration count then returns whichever branch the last iteration reached, and the
reported baseline flips with the parity of the count. Damping of 0.3 with a residual test
converges in all four configurations tested, so a convergence diagnostic should accompany any
droop baseline computed this way.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conc}

We computed an upper bound on multi-hub V2G voltage support for the IEEE 34-bus feeder and used
it to separate the shortfall due to control from the shortfall due to stored energy. Uneven
coordination across five hubs is of real value, clearing every violating hour at a mild peak
where a single hub of equal rating clears seven of eighteen. Once realistic availability and
state-of-charge limits are applied, however, the fleet supplies well under a fifth of the energy
the controller requests, and the resulting shortfall exceeds the entire span between a tuned
droop law and a converged learned policy by a factor of roughly 45 to 50.

This is not an argument against learned control in distribution systems, but an argument that
the operating regime should be established before the controller is designed, because in the
energy-limited regime a better policy has very little left to win. Both the upper bound and an
energy adequacy ratio follow from feeder and fleet data before any controller exists, and we
suggest reporting them as standard context alongside controller comparisons.

Two further results point the same way. A degradation-aware objective removes 60 percent of
battery throughput for a 1.1 percent violation penalty, because the marginal kilowatt-hour is
nearly worthless in this regime. A safety projection layer, standard elsewhere, is measurably
inert here because the energy constraint binds first.

The study covers one feeder and one learning algorithm, and the mild-peak comparison remains
unresolved at the budgets we could afford. The ratio reported here depends on feeder impedance
and hub siting, so it should be recomputed on a larger network. More usefully, the bound is
inexpensive enough to invert: rather than scoring a controller against it, it can serve as a
planning screen returning the fleet size and availability needed to make a target violation
level reachable.
