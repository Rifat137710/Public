\begin{abstract}
Sensitivity-based action projection has become the default way to enforce
voltage limits on a learned or heuristic EV charging controller: a linearised
power-flow model turns each request into the nearest request that keeps every
bus inside the band. The mechanism is widely reused and its \emph{refresh
requirement} is not reported anywhere. We measure it. On two unrelated radial
feeders---an IEEE 33-bus medium-voltage testbed and a 117-bus low-voltage
village benchmark---a projection whose linearisation base point is never
refreshed reproduces the \emph{unprojected} violation rate to within 3\,\%
(96.9--100.0\,\%, 12 of 12 cells) while reporting an infeasibility rate of
exactly zero. The layer does not degrade as its model ages; it becomes inert,
silently. The transition is a cliff rather than a slope, and its location is not
a universal constant: it sits at two hours on one feeder and one hour on the
other. Separately, we decompose the linearisation into its Jacobian and its base
point and perturb each independently. Across a station-bus Jacobian error
spanning $0.20\times$ to $5.67\times$, a network model wrong by up to a factor
of two costs nothing on either feeder, while under-estimating line impedance
drives the projection infeasible on 8.3\,\% of steps and latches stations to
zero on 6.9\,\%---producing a \emph{perfect} violation rate that the fallback,
not the projection, paid for. Both failure modes defeat the same check, from
opposite directions: a violation rate on its own cannot tell an operator whether
a safety layer is working. We give the resulting runtime diagnostic, the
measured tolerance bands, and the operating envelope.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Electric vehicle charging, voltage control, safe reinforcement learning, action

\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================

Uncoordinated EV charging depresses voltage at the ends of weak radial feeders,
and on low-voltage networks the effect appears at very low penetration. A large
recent literature responds by wrapping a controller---increasingly a
reinforcement learning agent---in a \emph{safety layer}: a small optimisation
that projects each requested set-point onto the nearest point satisfying a
linearised voltage constraint~\cite{dalal2018,aamas2023,kou2022,gan2023,bigat2026,evsafe2024}.
The construction descends from Dalal \emph{et al.}~\cite{dalal2018} and is now
one of the two top-level categories in the field's own taxonomy of safe
reinforcement learning for power systems~\cite{saferlreview}.

The linearisation at the heart of this mechanism has two parts that are almost
always treated as one. The \emph{Jacobian} $\partial V/\partial P$,
$\partial V/\partial Q$ requires a network model. The \emph{base point}---the
operating point about which that Jacobian was taken---requires a measurement.
They fail differently, they cost differently to maintain, and no published work
we are aware of separates them.

Nor does any of the work above state how often its linearisation is refreshed.
That is not a small omission. Distribution systems are described in their own
state-estimation literature as ``practically unobservable in real
time''~\cite{dsse_survey}, measurement-scarce, with heterogeneous reporting
rates, and readily rendered unobservable by communication failure or delay.
Distribution state estimation runs on 1--15\,minute cycles and AMI on 15\,minutes
to an hour; telemetry is concentrated at the substation rather than at the
station buses over which the constraint is written. A stale base point is not an
edge case on a distribution feeder. It is the ordinary condition.

\subsection{What is, and is not, new here}

We are explicit about the part that is not ours. That measurement feedback
substitutes for model accuracy is an established principle in at least three
literatures: incremental, sensor-based flight control replaces model terms with
direct measurements and is robust to model uncertainty as a
result~\cite{indi}; measurement-based (``model-less'') voltage control estimates
sensitivity coefficients from data precisely because model-based ones are
unreliable~\cite{modelless}; and online feedback-based linearised power flow
updates its parameters from instantaneous measurements and already reports
robustness to measurement error, communication failure and update
frequency~\cite{bai2022}. We reconfirm that principle rather than claim it.

What is not established is the \emph{closed-loop consequence}. The metric in
\cite{bai2022} is voltage estimation error---an open-loop accuracy measure. Ours
is whether a safety filter still refuses an unsafe action. These do not follow
from one another: a model can be badly wrong and still refuse unsafe requests,
or mildly wrong and pass everything. A linearisation that grows gradually less
accurate does not imply a filter that grows gradually less protective, and we
find it does not.

Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A silent-failure characterisation.} A never-refreshed projection
      reproduces the unprojected violation rate while reporting zero
      infeasibilities. The failure is total, and it raises no alarm on the
      channel an operator would watch (Sec.~\ref{sec:staleness}).
\item \textbf{A decomposition of the linearisation into Jacobian and base
      point}, each perturbed independently. The artefact that is expensive to
      obtain tolerates a factor-of-two error; the one constrained by metering
      infrastructure carries the protection (Sec.~\ref{sec:modelerror}).
\item \textbf{A runtime diagnostic.} Two unrelated failure modes each produce a
      clean violation rate. We give the telemetry that distinguishes a working
      layer from a broken one, as a one-line assertion
      (Sec.~\ref{sec:diagnostic}).
\item \textbf{Measured tolerance bands and an operating envelope}, on two
      feeders sharing no topology, voltage level or impedance scale.
\end{enumerate}

We also report, and do not hide, that on this testbed the projection rather than
the learned policy is what delivers safe service (Sec.~\ref{sec:layer}). That
result motivates studying the layer rather than the learner, and it is the
reason a learned controller appears here only as one of several request sources.

\section{Related Work}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{Safety layers for distribution voltage control.} Dalal \emph{et
al.}~\cite{dalal2018} introduce a layer that analytically corrects an action to
satisfy linearised constraints. The construction has been carried into
distribution voltage control in multi-agent~\cite{aamas2023}, Volt-VAR
\cite{kou2022}, communication-delayed~\cite{gan2023}, physics-regularised
\cite{bigat2026} and EV-charging~\cite{evsafe2024} settings. Two of these make
directly opposing claims about model sensitivity---\cite{aamas2023} advertises
that approximate sensitivity information suffices, \cite{bigat2026} motivates
itself by projection being sensitive to parameter error---and neither reports
the experiment that would settle it. Sec.~\ref{sec:modelerror} does.

\textbf{Sensitivity computation and currency.} Analytic sensitivity coefficients
for radial networks are standard~\cite{christakou2013}. Measurement-based
estimation~\cite{modelless} and online feedback linearisation~\cite{bai2022}
address model unavailability directly; \cite{bai2022} is the closest prior work
to ours and the distinction is the one drawn in Sec.~I: it measures how accurate
a linearisation remains, we measure whether a filter built on one still refuses
unsafe actions.

\textbf{Delay and staleness.} Communication delay has been treated as a
millisecond-scale synchronisation problem to be imputed away~\cite{gan2023}.
Age of information has been used as a formal staleness metric in power systems,
for transmission-level load frequency control~\cite{aoi_lfc}. We treat
hour-scale base-point age as a design variable for distribution constraint
enforcement, where the failure is inertness rather than instability.

\section{System Model and Safety Projection}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Feeder and fleet}

A radial feeder carries $N_b$ buses, of which four host bidirectional charging
stations rated $S^{\mathrm{rat}}$. AC power flow is solved every control step by
a backward--forward sweep validated against Newton--Raphson to $10^{-9}$\,p.u.
The control step is five minutes over a 288-step day. Vehicles arrive from a
bimodal profile with log-normal dwell times and per-vehicle energy targets; a
station's set-point is distributed across its connected vehicles in proportion
to energy still owed. We use the sign convention of the underlying
solver: $p>0$ denotes injection into the bus (V2G discharge) and $p<0$ denotes
charging.

The operational requirement is $\underline{v} \le v_i \le \overline{v}$ for all
$i$, with $[\underline{v},\overline{v}] = [0.95, 1.05]$\,p.u.

\subsection{The projection}

Let $u = (p^{\mathrm{req}}, q^{\mathrm{req}})$ be the requested station
set-points. The safety layer returns
%
\begin{align}
\min_{p,q} \quad & \lVert p - p^{\mathrm{req}} \rVert_2^2
                 + \lVert q - q^{\mathrm{req}} \rVert_2^2 \label{eq:proj}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \underline{v} + \varepsilon \le \hat v(p,q) \le \overline{v} - \varepsilon
  \nonumber\\
& \lVert (p_j, q_j) \rVert_2 \le S^{\mathrm{rat}}_j, \quad
  \lvert p_j - p_j^{-} \rvert \le \Delta^{\max} \nonumber
\end{align}
%
where $\varepsilon$ is a tightening margin, $p^{-}$ the previous set-point, and
the predicted voltage is the linearisation
%
\begin{equation}
\hat v(p,q) = \underbrace{v^{0}}_{\text{base point}}
  + \underbrace{\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}}_{\text{Jacobian}}(p - p^{0})
  + \frac{\partial V}{\partial Q}(q - q^{0}).
\label{eq:lin}
\end{equation}
%
Problem~\eqref{eq:proj} is a second-order cone program, solved with CLARABEL
through a DPP-compliant parametrisation so the problem is compiled once and
re-solved with new parameters each step. A solution is accepted on its
\emph{measured} constraint residual rather than on the solver's status label,
because a hedged-but-feasible point is common and discarding it would freeze
stations for nothing.

\subsection{The two halves of the linearisation}
\label{sec:twohalves}

Equation~\eqref{eq:lin} is the object of this paper. The Jacobian
$\partial V/\partial P$, $\partial V/\partial Q$ is a property of the network:
obtaining it requires an admittance model, and it changes only when the network
does. The base point $(v^{0}, p^{0}, q^{0})$ is a property of the present
operating condition: obtaining it requires a \emph{measurement}, and it changes
continuously.

This asymmetry is the reason the decomposition matters in deployment. The
Jacobian can be computed offline as often as one likes---we compute it
analytically from a single LU factorisation of the power-flow Jacobian, in
0.662\,ms against 447.6\,ms for finite differences. A current base point cannot
be computed at all. It must be observed, at whatever cadence the metering
infrastructure provides. A designer therefore cannot buy base-point currency
with compute, which is exactly why its requirement needs to be known.

We denote by $\tau$ the \emph{base-point age}: the number of control steps since
the linearisation was last taken. Throughout, ``refresh interval $\tau$'' means
the Jacobian and base point are recomputed every $\tau$ steps and held constant
in between, which is what a controller with periodic state updates does.

\subsection{Infeasibility fallback}

When~\eqref{eq:proj} is infeasible the station holds its previous set-point;
after three consecutive failures it latches to zero. This is disclosed rather
than defended---Sec.~\ref{sec:diagnostic} shows it becoming the dominant
mechanism under model error, which is a finding about the design, not an
endorsement of it.

\section{Experimental Design}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Two feeders}

Generality cannot be claimed from one set of line impedances, so every result
below is measured on two networks that share nothing (Table~\ref{tab:testbeds}).
The MV testbed is the IEEE 33-bus Baran--Wu feeder with a Th\'evenin source
impedance $Z_{\mathrm{sub}}$ added upstream of the slack, giving 34 buses. The
LV testbed is the Kerber \emph{Dorfnetz} German village benchmark: 116 buses,
six laterals, 57 households behind a 400\,kVA transformer at 0.4\,kV, with line
impedances an order of magnitude smaller. Its distribution transformer is folded
into an equivalent series line---exact for a fixed tap---and line shunt
capacitance is neglected, which we measured to move $V_{\min}$ by
$2.2\times10^{-5}$\,p.u., three orders of magnitude below the margin
$\varepsilon$.

\subsection{Operating point, chosen by measurement}

An operating point selected for convenience can trivialise a study in either
direction, so both feeders' operating points are measured against two
requirements: the \emph{idle} feeder must be strictly inside the band, so that
every violation is attributable to a charging decision; and an uncoordinated
charger must break the band, so the layer has something to do. On the LV feeder
we swept 36 combinations of substation stiffness, background load and fleet
size, and selected the cell closest to the MV feeder's uncoordinated behaviour
on \emph{both} axes simultaneously---a second feeder tuned harder would inflate
the effect and one tuned easier would hide it.

\subsection{Request sources}

A claim about a safety layer should not depend on what is upstream of it. We
therefore drive the layer from unrelated sources: an uncoordinated greedy
charger, a deadline-aware heuristic that defers non-urgent stations, and (on the
MV feeder) a trained soft actor--critic agent with an augmented-Lagrangian cost
constraint.

\subsection{Metrics}

We report the \emph{voltage violation step rate}---the fraction of control steps
with any bus outside the band---and \emph{service}, the fraction of vehicles
meeting their energy target at departure. Crucially we also report the layer's
own telemetry: the fraction of steps on which~\eqref{eq:proj} was infeasible and
the fraction on which a station was latched to zero. Sec.~\ref{sec:diagnostic}
is about why the first two are not sufficient.

Seeds are derived by hashing run labels, with disjoint training and evaluation
bands. The implementation carries 90 tests and reproduces the reference results
of the underlying testbed exactly.

\section{Results}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{The layer, not the learner}
\label{sec:layer}

Before asking what the layer requires, we establish that it---rather than the
policy above it---is what delivers safe service. The projection is usually
composed only with a learned policy, which conflates two questions: whether the
layer makes actions safe, and whether learning adds anything on top of it.
Applying the identical projection to a zero-intelligence greedy charger
separates them (Table~\ref{tab:layer}).

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{The same projection, different controllers (MV feeder).}
\label{tab:layer}
\centering\small
\input{tables/layer_not_learner}
\end{table}

The projection applied to a controller with no learning at all delivers
$8.0\times$ the service of the projection applied to the trained agent, at
identical zero violations. A sweep over background load and EV penetration found
no cell in which deferring non-urgent stations improved on charging everything:
on this testbed deferred energy is never recovered, so the binding resource is
energy over the day rather than its allocation across stations, and there is no
scheduling structure for a policy to exploit. \emph{This is a statement about
this testbed at this training budget, not a claim that learning cannot help.} It
is why the remainder of the paper studies the layer, and why a learned policy
appears below only as one request source among several.

\subsection{Base-point staleness}
\label{sec:staleness}

We now hold the network model correct and vary only $\tau$
(Table~\ref{tab:staleness}).

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Violation step rate against base-point age. Correct network model
throughout; only the linearisation's currency varies. Bold marks zero.}
\label{tab:staleness}
\centering\scriptsize
\input{tables/staleness}
\end{table}

Three things stand out.

\textbf{The layer switches off rather than degrading.} At $\tau = 288$---one
refresh per day, i.e.\ never after the first solve---the violation rate is the
\emph{unprojected} rate, recovering 96.9--100.0\,\% of it across all twelve
cells and both request sources. A base point captured on an idle feeder makes
the constraint appear slack at every subsequent step, the feasibility pre-check
skips every solve, and every request passes through untouched.

\textbf{It is silent while doing so.} The infeasibility rate at $\tau=288$ is
$0.0000$ in every cell. The layer is not failing to solve; it never solves. An
operator monitoring the component's own fault channel sees nothing.

\textbf{The transition is a cliff, and its location is not a constant.} Between
one interval and the next, protection goes from complete to essentially absent.
On the MV feeder at $Z_{\mathrm{sub}}=6\,\%$ the last clean interval is 24 steps
(two hours); on the LV feeder it is 12 steps (one hour), identically across a
threefold stiffness range and both request sources. We therefore do not offer a
universal refresh figure. \emph{The cliff exists everywhere we looked; its
location varies with the deployment and must be measured for one.} We also do
not claim to know what sets it: on the MV feeder it tracks substation stiffness,
on the LV feeder it does not move at all.

\subsection{Model error}
\label{sec:modelerror}

We now hold the base point current---measured on the real feeder at every
step---and corrupt only the Jacobian, by computing it on a feeder whose line
impedances are scaled by a factor $k$. This is the arrangement a deployed
controller with an out-of-date network model but live telemetry actually
occupies. Scaling impedances, rather than changing feeders, keeps the Jacobian's
dimensions matched while moving its magnitude over
$0.20\times$--$5.67\times$ (Table~\ref{tab:modelerror}).

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Violation step rate against Jacobian error, base point measured
throughout. Bold marks zero.}
\label{tab:modelerror}
\centering\scriptsize
\input{tables/model_error}
\end{table}

A Jacobian wrong by up to roughly a factor of two costs nothing: violations are
zero across $0.80\times$--$2.06\times$ on both feeders and both request sources.
Set against Sec.~\ref{sec:staleness}, the asymmetry is the paper's central
practical point. The half of the linearisation that is expensive to obtain, and
that a utility may not have, tolerates substantial error. The half that cannot
be computed at all---only measured---carries the protection.

Outside the tolerance band the failure is directional, and which direction is
feeder-dependent: the LV feeder leaks violations when impedance is
under-estimated, the MV feeder when it is over-estimated. Over-estimation is the
conservative side, and it is not free either---on the LV feeder a $5.5\times$
Jacobian holds violations at zero but costs 12 percentage points of service.

\subsection{Zero is not evidence: a runtime diagnostic}
\label{sec:diagnostic}

The two failure modes above are mirror images, and Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic}
puts them side by side.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Two failure modes. Neither is visible in the column an operator
watches.}
\label{tab:diagnostic}
\centering\small
\input{tables/diagnostic}
\end{table}

A stale base point produces a clean \emph{infeasibility} rate while the feeder
is entirely unprotected. An under-estimated impedance produces a clean
\emph{violation} rate---on the MV feeder, exactly zero, apparently
perfect---while the projection is infeasible on 8.3\,\% of steps and the
fallback has latched stations to zero on 6.9\,\%, costing four percentage points
of service. In the second case the layer is not protecting the feeder; it is
switching the load off and being credited for the result.

Neither is detectable from the outcome alone. We therefore recommend that a
deployed projection expose, and an operator alarm on, three quantities rather
than one:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Base-point age $\tau$}, alarmed against a threshold measured for
      that feeder rather than assumed.
\item \textbf{Solve rate.} A projection reporting zero infeasibilities
      \emph{and} a near-zero solve rate on a loaded feeder has stopped binding;
      it is not succeeding, it is absent.
\item \textbf{Fallback activation.} Non-trivial latch rates mean the reported
      violation rate is being paid for by curtailment, not by projection.
\end{enumerate}

The first two together are a one-line assertion any of the works in
Sec.~II could adopt.

\subsection{Cost}

The analytic sensitivity computation runs in 0.662\,ms against 447.6\,ms for
finite differences, and a full projection step costs on the order of
milliseconds against a 300\,s control interval---a duty cycle of roughly
0.004\,\%. Every refresh interval examined here is computationally free. The
constraint on base-point currency is metering, not computation, which is the
practical reading of Sec.~\ref{sec:modelerror}.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{Scope.} These results are for weak, low-short-circuit-ratio radial
feeders, where voltage rather than thermal loading is the binding constraint.
On robustly built distribution networks the reported impact of EV charging on
voltage is small and transformer thermal limits bind first; we do not claim
otherwise, and our testbeds are chosen accordingly---the LV feeder sits at
$V_{\min}=0.9550$\,p.u.\ before any vehicle is connected.

\textbf{What ``refresh'' means here.} We simulate base-point age by recomputing
the power flow at interval $\tau$, not by simulating a metering and
communication pipeline. The axis is the \emph{age of the base point}, agnostic
to what produces it. Whether real deployments occupy the unsafe region is an
open question this paper does not answer; we note only that the LV feeder's
cliff at one hour coincides with typical AMI reporting intervals.

\textbf{Statistics.} We report measured rates over 25 evaluation episodes per
cell and do not claim confidence intervals. The effects reported are of the form
$0.0000$ against $0.0642$; the sub-percentage-point differences we observed
between short refresh intervals were of opposite sign on the two feeders, and we
therefore make no claim about them.

\textbf{Request sources.} Three sources were used on the MV feeder and two on
the LV feeder.

\textbf{Future work.} The natural next step is to make $\tau$ an explicit
argument of the constraint, tightening the band by a data-driven bound on net
load drift over $\tau$ so that protection degrades continuously instead of
switching off. The construction exists in the control literature for systems
with sporadic measurements~\cite{sporadiccbf}; adapting it to an AC power-flow
band, with a drift bound estimated from measured load rather than assumed
adversarial, is the subject of ongoing work.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

A sensitivity-based safety layer's protection rests on the currency of its
linearisation base point rather than the accuracy of its network model. Wrong by
a factor of two, the model costs nothing; a base point that stops being
refreshed removes the protection entirely, reproducing the unprojected violation
rate while reporting no fault. The transition is a cliff whose location varies
between deployments, and two distinct failure modes both present as a clean
number in the column an operator would watch. The remedy is not a faster refresh
rate---every interval we examined is computationally free---but instrumentation:
measure the base-point age, the solve rate and the fallback activation, and
alarm on all three.
