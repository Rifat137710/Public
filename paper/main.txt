Voltage Support Capability of Multi-Hub Vehicle-to-Grid Operation Under Fleet Energy Constraints

Md. Rifat Rahman, Shaikh Anowarul Fattah, Senior Member, IEEE
Department of Electrical & Electronic Engineering
Bangladesh University of Engineering & Technology, Dhaka, Bangladesh
Email: 2006137@eee.buet.ac.bd

ABSTRACT
--------

Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) operation is often proposed as a voltage support resource on weak
distribution feeders. Recent studies compare learned controllers against local droop
control, but they do not establish how much support the connected fleet can actually
provide. This paper computes an upper bound on that support. For every hour of a daily
load profile, the active and reactive injection of each hub is optimized directly on the
AC power flow, limited only by inverter rating. The bound is then used to separate two
effects: the shortfall caused by limited stored energy, and the shortfall caused by the
controller. On the IEEE 34-bus feeder, coordinated dispatch across five hubs clears
every violating hour at a mild peak, while a single hub of the same rating clears fewer
than half. Under realistic availability and state-of-charge limits, however, the fleet
supplies less than 15 percent of the energy the controller requests. Energy adequacy
outweighs controller choice by more than a factor of 40 at both load levels. Fleet
sizing and availability therefore deserve more attention than controller sophistication.

I. INTRODUCTION
---------------

Long rural distribution feeders were not built for the voltage profiles they must now
hold. Peak demand pulls the far end of the circuit below acceptable limits, and the
usual remedies, such as reconductoring and additional regulators, are slow and
expensive. Bidirectional inverters installed with electric vehicle (EV) fleets offer
another option. They sit near the load end of the feeder, where support is most
effective, and their cost is borne by the fleet owner. Recent work applies reinforcement
learning (RL) to coordinate several V2G hubs for voltage regulation on the IEEE 34-bus
feeder [1].

A wider body of work applies RL to inverter dispatch for the same purpose. Safe off-
policy methods [2], multi-agent consensus formulations [3], and graph-based
architectures with explicit safety layers [4] have all been reported. Related studies
manage EV charging to hold voltage in low-voltage networks [5] and examine battery-to-
grid injection from charging and swapping stations [6]. EV stock continues to grow [7],
and with it the capacity notionally available for such services. Across this literature
the controller is treated as the binding constraint, so that a better policy is expected
to extract more support from the same hardware.

That assumption has not been tested. None of these studies computes what the fleet could
deliver under an ideal controller, so a reported improvement cannot be placed on a
scale. A policy that halves the violation of a droop baseline may have captured most of
the available benefit, or very little of it, and the published numbers do not separate
the two cases.

This paper computes the missing reference point. For each hour we solve for the per-hub
active and reactive injection that minimizes a two-sided voltage violation metric, using
the AC power flow and limiting only inverter rating. No energy constraint is applied, so
the result is an upper bound set by the power electronics alone. Comparing it against
droop and learned controllers, run with and without a realistic fleet model, splits the
total shortfall into an energy part and a control part.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:

  - We compute an hourly upper bound on multi-hub V2G voltage support for the IEEE
    34-bus feeder and use it to split the shortfall into energy and control parts.

  - We measure the active and reactive trade-off per unit of stored energy rather than
    per unit of apparent power.

  - We compare droop and learned control under realistic availability and state-of-
    charge limits, and sweep the training budget to five times its usual value.

  - We report a degradation-aware dispatch frontier and three negative results.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II describes the feeder,
the fleet model, the metrics and the controllers. Section III computes the upper bound
and splits the shortfall. Section IV reports controller performance, Section V the
degradation frontier, Section VI three further observations, and Section VII concludes.

II. SYSTEM MODEL AND STUDY DESIGN
---------------------------------

A. Feeder and Charging Hubs

All experiments use the IEEE 34-bus test feeder, a 24.9 kV rural circuit whose length
makes it a standard stress case for voltage studies, solved in OpenDSS through its
Python interface. The single-hub configuration places one 500 kW/400 kVAr hub at bus
890. The multi-hub configuration distributes five hubs of the same rating at buses 890,
844, 832, 830 and 860, following [1]. A daily load shape drives hours 06:00 through
23:00, scaled by a peak multiplier. Two operating points are used throughout: a mild
peak (lambda_max=1.5) and an aggressive peak (lambda_max=3.0).

With the regulators disabled, our minimum voltages fall within 0.004 to 0.007 p.u. of
the reported values. With them active, the mild minimum differs by 0.076 p.u., which is
outside any plausible modelling difference. Our feeder mean runs 0.012 to 0.023 p.u.
low, which we attribute to load allocation details not given in the reference, so the
configuration is identified from the minimum rather than the mean. All later results use
disabled regulators.

C. Fleet and Battery Model

Each hub serves a fleet whose availability varies through the day between 45 and 85
percent and is drawn independently per scenario. Vehicles arrive at 70 percent state of
charge and are not discharged below 20 percent. State of charge is drawn down by
apparent power, so reactive support costs the same stored energy as active support. This
follows the reference formulation and matters for Section III-C.

The distinction between an unconstrained and a constrained fleet runs through the whole
study. The reference work evaluates multi-hub coordination while "assuming sufficient EV
capacity at each hub, allowing the analysis to isolate the benefits of multi-hub
coordination from availability constraints" [1]. We report both cases, because the
difference between them turns out to be the principal result.

D. Evaluation Metrics

ANSI C84.1 [8] is a per-phase standard, so voltages are evaluated per phase rather than
as bus averages, which mask single-phase excursions on this unbalanced feeder. The
primary metric is the day-integrated two-sided violation

    IV = sum_h sum_p [ max(0, v_min - v_ph) + max(0, v_ph - v_max) ] dt

in per-unit-hours, with v_min=0.95, v_max=1.05 and dt = 1 h. Unlike a count of violation
hours, (1) does not saturate when two controllers violate in the same hours by different
margins, and being two-sided it penalizes overvoltage as well.

All comparisons use common random numbers. Every controller sees the same availability
draw, initial state of charge and load realization, and differences are paired.
Confidence intervals are 95 percent over the paired scenarios and training seeds.

E. Control Algorithms

The droop baseline is a Volt-Watt and Volt-VAr characteristic with a +/- 0.02 p.u.
deadband, consistent with IEEE 1547 [9]. Droop response and feeder voltage are mutually
dependent, so the baseline is not evaluated open-loop. We obtain the fixed point by
damped Jacobi updates with a residual convergence test, a detail examined in Section VI.

The learned controller is soft actor-critic [10] as implemented in Stable-Baselines3
[11], with two hidden layers of 256 units, a learning rate of 3x10^-4 and a batch size
of 256. Episodes span one day, so that rationing of a finite battery over time is
representable. The state comprises bus voltages, load multiplier, hour of day, and per-
hub state of charge and availability. The action sets per-hub active and reactive power
as a residual on the droop prior.

III. VOLTAGE SUPPORT CAPABILITY
-------------------------------

A. Upper Bound on Voltage Support

For each hour we search for the per-hub injection (P_i, Q_i) that minimizes (1) subject
to P_i^2+Q_i^2 <= S_rated. The search is a coordinate descent over a polar grid with
refinement, and a full AC power flow is evaluated at every candidate. No energy
constraint is imposed. Non-converged solutions are masked rather than read, because at
deep-sag hours the high-injection end of the search lies past the nose of the power-
voltage curve.

Searching per hub rather than uniformly is necessary. With five hubs at equal output,
raising the feeder end to 0.95 p.u. drives the near buses above 1.05 p.u., so hours that
an uneven dispatch clears comfortably are scored as unclearable. Under uniform dispatch
the five-hub case then scores worse than the single-hub case, which is not physically
possible. Per-hub search removes the artefact and moves the mild multi-hub result from
five clearable hours to eighteen.

Table II reports the outcome. A single hub at full rating clears seven of the eighteen
loaded hours at the mild peak and two at the aggressive peak. Five hubs of the same
individual rating, dispatched unevenly, clear all eighteen and four respectively. Fig. 2
shows the hourly picture. The reachable envelope sits on or above the 0.95 p.u. limit
for the entire mild day, and falls short of it for eleven hours of the aggressive day
regardless of dispatch. Those eleven hours are a siting and rating problem, not a
control problem, and no policy can clear them. At equal total inverter rating, spreading
capacity across five hubs and dispatching it unevenly reduces violation by 100 percent
at the mild peak and by 36.5 percent at the aggressive peak, relative to uniform
dispatch.

B. Energy and Control Contributions

Fig. 1 places the controllers against this bound and is the central result of the paper.
Reading the mild panel, the untreated feeder accumulates 45.61 p.u.h of violation and
the bound removes all of it. A droop law with unlimited stored energy removes 41.46, or
91 percent, while the same law with a realistic fleet removes 2.84, or 6 percent. The
fleet delivers 1342 kWh against the 9264 kWh the unconstrained law requests, a ratio of
14.5 percent. At the aggressive peak the ratio is 5.7 percent.

The controller span lies inside that shortfall. Between tuned droop and the best learned
policy we obtained, the difference is 0.79 p.u.h at the mild peak and 2.58 at the
aggressive peak, against energy shortfalls of 38.62 and 115.69. Energy adequacy
therefore outweighs controller choice by a factor of 49 and 45.

C. Active and Reactive Trade-off

Because state of charge is drawn down by apparent power, the common assumption that
reactive power is free does not hold. Sweeping the injection angle at fixed apparent
power and fixed loading, we find that active power delivers between 1.25 and 1.49 times
the worst-phase voltage rise of reactive power for the same battery energy withdrawn.
The voltage-optimal split is 35 to 40 degrees from the active axis at half rating, and
moves towards 17 to 26 degrees at full rating as the resistive voltage drop becomes
dominant. The hub rating ratio of 400 kVAr to 500 kW corresponds to 38.7 degrees.

This carries a design consequence. On a feeder with this R/X character, a hub sized by
apparent power and operated at its rating ratio commits more reactive power than is
useful, and pays full battery energy for it.

IV. CONTROLLER PERFORMANCE UNDER FLEET CONSTRAINTS
--------------------------------------------------

A. Multi-Hub Operation

Table III reports the five-hub feeder with the fleet model active, over three seeds and
five paired scenarios. Droop reaches 42.77 p.u.h at the mild peak and 165.83 at the
aggressive peak. The learned policy trained on the conventional 20,000-step budget
reaches 45.45 and 169.60, and loses to droop in every paired scenario at both load
levels.

Two features of the table are worth noting. The learned policy consumes 5842 kWh of
battery throughput at the mild peak against 1342 kWh for droop, and converts that into a
worse voltage outcome. At the mild peak its result is statistically indistinguishable
from installing no V2G at all. Both controllers respect the upper voltage limit exactly,
at 1.050 p.u., a point taken up in Section VI.

B. Training Analysis

A negative result about a learned controller invites the objection that it was not
trained long enough. The objection is reasonable, since 20,000 steps is about 1100
episodes and 19,000 gradient updates for a ten-dimensional action space. We therefore
trained to 100,000 steps and evaluated at 20,000-step checkpoints on identical
scenarios. Checkpoints fall on the same chunk boundaries the fixed-budget runs use, so
the 20,000-step row measures the same agent reported in Table III.

The outcome differs by load level, as Fig. 3 shows. At the aggressive peak the curve is
flat from 40,000 steps onward. Violation moves from 169.20 to 168.41 across a fivefold
increase in budget, which lies inside the combined confidence interval of 1.13, and
droop wins every paired scenario at every checkpoint. The advantage of droop under
aggressive stress is therefore not an artefact of training budget.

At the mild peak the curve is still improving at 100,000 steps. Violation falls from
45.05 to 43.56, a change of -1.48 against a confidence interval of 0.49. The gap to
droop narrows monotonically from +2.27 to +0.79, and the policy takes two of ten paired
scenarios, the only scenarios it wins anywhere in this study. We therefore report the
mild comparison as unresolved at the budgets tested rather than as a droop victory. It
is worth setting that improvement against the previous section: the gap narrowed by 1.48
p.u.h over a fivefold increase in training, against an energy shortfall of 38.62 at the
same operating point.

Fig. 3. Violation against training budget.

C. Allocation Analysis

A scoreboard alone does not explain the outcome, so we examined the active and reactive
split the policy commands, hour by hour, weighted by apparent power and compared against
the voltage-optimal angle recomputed at the injection level the policy itself selected.

The policy does not track the optimum, and the deviation does not fall with more
training. At 20,000 steps the mean absolute deviation is 26.5 degrees at the mild peak
and 32.5 degrees at the aggressive peak. At 100,000 steps these become 20.8 and 31.5
degrees. The changes of -5.7 and -1.0 degrees both lie inside their combined 95 percent
confidence intervals of 24.2 and 35.1 degrees, so neither is a reduction the data
supports.

Fig. 4 shows the hourly picture at 100,000 steps. The voltage-optimal angle stays
between 29 and 35 degrees for most of the day at both load levels. At the mild peak the
commanded angle stays near 20 degrees, with an apparent-power-weighted daily mean of
18.8 degrees. At the aggressive peak the daily mean falls to 1.2 degrees, which is
almost pure active power, and in 50 percent of sampled hub-hours the commanded reactive
power is negative. Reactive absorption lowers voltage on this feeder, so these are
actions of the wrong sign for the condition being corrected, not merely conservative
ones. The pattern is consistent with a controller that scales a roughly fixed power
factor instead of allocating between active and reactive power in response to loading.

Fig. 4. Commanded injection angle against the voltage-optimal angle, by hour.

V. DEGRADATION-AWARE DISPATCH
-----------------------------

Battery wear is the standing objection to V2G participation, and the economic case is
sensitive to it [12]. The reference formulation carries no battery term in its reward.
We added an amp-hour throughput cost, expressed on the same scale as the voltage
penalty, and swept its weight across three orders of magnitude.

The resulting frontier is flat, as Fig. 5 shows. Across a 300-fold sweep the violation
stays within a 2.6 percent band, between 44.83 and 46.01 per-unit-hours, while daily
throughput falls from 6188 to 2449 kWh. Comparing the endpoints, roughly 60 percent of
battery throughput can be removed for a 1.1 percent increase in violation.

This follows from the energy-limited regime identified earlier. When the binding
constraint is that the fleet cannot supply what the feeder needs, the marginal voltage
value of the last kilowatt-hour discharged is very low, so a wear penalty removes it at
almost no cost. The practical reading is favourable. On feeders in this regime,
aggressive degradation-aware dispatch costs little in voltage performance, which
improves the participation case for fleet owners.

The intermediate points should not be over-interpreted. Each weight was trained with a
single seed and the sequence is not monotone, so the defensible claims are the endpoint
comparison and the width of the band, not the shape in between.

VI. ADDITIONAL OBSERVATIONS
---------------------------

This section reports three checks that did not produce the expected effect. Each
addresses a design choice that is common in the literature.

A. Safety Projection

Projecting commanded setpoints onto the voltage-feasible set is standard practice in
learning-based volt/var control [2, 4], so its absence would be a reasonable objection.
We implemented and measured two projections: a proportional reduction along the
commanded ray, and a reactive-first variant motivated by Section III-C.

Both are inert. The unprojected policy already reports zero overvoltage and a maximum
phase voltage of exactly 1.050 p.u. at both load levels, and projection changes the
violation metric by less than the confidence interval. The feasibility test acts on
commanded setpoints, but the state-of-charge limit clips those commands first. The
energy constraint binds before the voltage constraint does, leaving nothing for a
voltage projection to remove. Where overvoltage does appear in our runs, it tracks the
training regime rather than the controller: policies trained on independently sampled
load multipliers without a fleet in the loop reach 1.229 p.u., whereas day-structured
training with the fleet in the loop holds 1.050 across every configuration and
checkpoint tested.

Fig. 5. Violation against battery throughput as the wear weight is swept from 0 to 300.

B. Fleet State

Adding state of charge and availability to the observation, information the reference
formulation does not provide, does not improve voltage performance. At the mild peak the
augmented agent reaches 45.41+/-0.36 against 44.91+/-0.29 for a voltage-only
observation, an overlapping interval. What it changes is energy use. The augmented agent
spends 6068 kWh against 2497 kWh for no violation benefit, while the voltage-only agent
breaches the upper limit at the aggressive peak where the augmented agent does not.

C. Droop Convergence

The last observation is methodological and affects any study using a closed-loop droop
baseline. Droop response and feeder voltage are mutually dependent, and the fixed point
is commonly computed with a fixed iteration count. On the five-hub feeder, at a damping
factor of 0.6, the iteration does not settle but enters a period-two limit cycle, with
total active power alternating between 1298 and 630 kW at the mild-peak evening hour. A
fixed iteration count then returns whichever branch the last iteration reached, and the
reported baseline flips with the parity of the count. Damping of 0.3 with a residual
test converges in all four configurations tested. A convergence diagnostic should
therefore accompany any droop baseline computed this way.

VII. CONCLUSION
---------------

We computed an upper bound on multi-hub V2G voltage support for the IEEE 34-bus feeder
and used it to separate the shortfall due to control from the shortfall due to stored
energy. Uneven coordination across five hubs is of real value, clearing every violating
hour at a mild peak where a single hub of equal rating clears seven of eighteen. Once
realistic availability and state-of-charge limits are applied, however, the fleet
supplies well under a fifth of the energy the controller requests, and the resulting
shortfall exceeds the entire span between a tuned droop law and a converged learned
policy by a factor of roughly 45 to 50.

This is not an argument against learned control in distribution systems. It is an
argument that the operating regime should be established before the controller is
designed, because in the energy-limited regime a better policy has very little left to
win. Both the upper bound and an energy adequacy ratio can be computed from feeder and
fleet data before any controller exists, and we suggest they be reported as standard
context alongside controller comparisons.

Two further results point the same way. A degradation-aware objective removes 60 percent
of battery throughput for a 1.1 percent violation penalty, because the marginal
kilowatt-hour is nearly worthless in this regime. A safety projection layer, standard
elsewhere, is measurably inert here because the energy constraint binds first.

The study covers one feeder and one learning algorithm, and the mild-peak comparison
remains unresolved at the budgets we could afford. Two extensions follow. The ratio
reported here depends on feeder impedance and hub siting, so it should be recomputed on
a larger feeder. More usefully, the bound is inexpensive enough to invert: rather than
scoring a controller against it, it can serve as a planning screen returning the fleet
size and availability needed to make a target violation level reachable.
