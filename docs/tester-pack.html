Run one session, eight times

Everything needed to sit a tester down, run them through the trainer, and come away with data that means something. Read the alarm on this page before you run the first one — a single careless sentence destroys the study's most valuable measurement.

Target 8–10 testers Per session ~32 min Needs a laptop, this pack, a pen Collects pre/post score, the pick, SUS

Read this first

Never mention that stage 6 is a trap

The single most valuable number in this study is how many testers deploy the controller that serves nobody. It only counts if they walk into it honestly.

So: do not say "trap", "trick", "be careful", "look closely", or "are you sure?". Do not raise an eyebrow. If they ask "is this a trick question?", answer "Just pick the one you'd actually deploy." and nothing more. If you slip, write it on the sheet — a contaminated session is still usable data as long as you know it was contaminated.

Before the first session

Set the laptop up once and reuse it. Open https://rifat137710.github.io/Public/ in a normal browser window, full screen, sound off. Reload between testers so each one starts at stage 1 with an empty map. Print one copy of the two instrument pages per tester; the script you keep.

Recruit engineers or engineering students — the point is to show the misconception exists in the population being taught, so people who already know power systems are the right audience, not the wrong one.

The session

00:002 min

Consent and framing

"This is a browser simulator about running an electricity grid with a lot of electric cars on it. I'll ask you five questions before, you'll play for about fifteen minutes, then the same five questions after. Nothing is recorded, nothing has your name on it, and you can stop at any point. There's no right answer I'm looking for — if it's confusing, that's a fact about my design, not about you."

Do not explain voltage, feeders, or what the sliders do. The trainer has to teach that itself; if you teach it, the pre/post delta measures you.

Pre-test — the five questions

Hand them the question sheet. Let them write. Say "say 'don't know' if you don't know, it's genuinely useful" — a blank and a guess score the same, but knowing which it was helps.

Do not react to answers. No nodding, no "good". Score later, not now.

07:0012 min

Stages 1 to 5 — they drive, then they watch

"Read the card, then do what it says. Think out loud if you can — tell me what you're looking at and what you expect to happen."

Stage 1 is the one that matters most: they run the feeder themselves with no help and almost certainly fail. Let them fail. If they ask what to do, say "whatever you think is right — you'll find out in a minute."

They can speed the day up with the ×16 and ×64 buttons. Point that out once if they haven't found it by stage 2.

Instrument 1 — the five concept questions

Asked identically before and after. Score 0–2 each, 0–10 total, marked afterwards against the rubric — never in front of the tester.

Q1

When an electricity feeder is heavily loaded, what happens to the voltage along it — and which quantity drives that change?

2 — voltage falls, and it falls further the further you are from the substation; driven by current through line impedance. 1 — voltage falls, no mechanism or no distance dependence. 0 — voltage rises, unchanged, or don't know.

Q2

On a weak rural feeder where resistance and reactance are roughly equal, does active power or reactive power have more effect on voltage? Why?

2 — active, because with R ≈ X the R·ΔP term is comparable to or larger than X·ΔQ, unlike transmission where X ≫ R. 1 — active, without the R ≈ X reasoning. 0 — reactive, or don't know.

Q3

A control system reports the lowest violation rate of every method tested. What would you check before trusting that number?

2 — what it cost in service — whether it achieved safety by doing its job well or by not operating. 1 — generic scepticism: test conditions, sample size, cherry-picking. 0 — nothing to check, or don't know. This is the question the entry exists to move.

Q4

Name two reasons a grid operator might refuse a request to send power from a parked car back into the network.

2 — any two of: it would push a bus above the upper voltage limit; the car's battery is at its floor; the driver needs the charge; thermal or equipment limits; battery degradation cost. 1 — one valid reason. 0 — none, or don't know.

Q5

Why might a controller that worked well on a strong urban grid fail when deployed on a weak rural one?

2 — it learned on a system where its actions barely moved voltage, so its policy is calibrated to the wrong sensitivity; on a weak feeder the same action has a much larger effect. 1 — "the conditions are different" without the mechanism. 0 — it wouldn't, or don't know.

Instrument 2 — SUS

The standard ten-item System Usability Scale, unmodified except that "the system" is named. 1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree. Hand it over; do not read the items aloud.

Statement1   2   3   4   5
1I think I would like to use Bus 18 frequently.
2I found Bus 18 unnecessarily complex.
3I thought Bus 18 was easy to use.
4I think I would need the support of a technical person to use Bus 18.
5I found the various functions in Bus 18 were well integrated.
6I thought there was too much inconsistency in Bus 18.
7I would imagine most people would learn to use Bus 18 very quickly.
8I found Bus 18 very cumbersome to use.
9I felt very confident using Bus 18.
10I needed to learn a lot before I could get going with Bus 18.

Scoring. Odd items: subtract 1 from the answer. Even items: subtract the answer from 5. Sum all ten, multiply by 2.5. Range 0–100. 68 is average; above 80 is good. Report the mean across testers and the range, never a single tester's score.

Honesty rule

Report what happened, including if nothing did

If the pre/post delta is small, report the small delta. If most testers see through stage 6 immediately, that is a real result and the entry says so. A study that can only come out one way is not a study, and a judge who has run one will know.
