Eight stated objectives (§8). The central one: a controller that reports the lowest violation rate can be the worst controller on the list — because it satisfied the constraint by refusing to operate. They learn to demand the service column.

Section 02What we are actually building

Bus 18 is a browser-based simulator in which one person plays the distribution operator of a rural feeder for one simulated day, then watches five automated controllers attempt the same day, and discovers by their own hand which of them is lying about being safe.

It is named after Baran–Wu bus 18 — the station at the electrically weakest point of the feeder, the one your projection curtails from −80 kW to −54.13 kW while the strong grid passes the same request untouched. That single number is the entire reference study in miniature, and it is the moment the simulator is built to deliver.

Is it a "metaverse"?

The competition's own FAQ 9 settles this, and it is worth quoting exactly because it removes the temptation to spend a week on a game engine:

FAQ 9 — verbatim

"Simulations can be developed in 2D or 3D environments and may run on desktop, web, mobile, or immersive platforms (e.g. VR/AR). The key evaluation focus is on the interactivity, innovation, and learning effectiveness, not the platform."

So the platform costs us nothing. What we do owe the word "metaverse" is earned three ways, and each is cheap:

  • A persistent place. The village is a continuous spatial world with real geography — the electrical distance from the substation is the visual distance across the screen. Voltage is rendered as light, so the physics is the landscape.
  • An embodied role. You are not reading about an operator. You hold the set-points, and the lights go out on your watch.
  • A shared record. The Pareto map accumulates dots. Your tester cohort's real attempts ship with the build as data, so every later visitor — including a judge — sees their own decision land inside a real population of prior decisions.
The four 2025 winners all built VR, because their subject was physical procedure training — hands, needles, wiring. Ours is a misconception about numbers. A headset would add cost, exclude viewers, and teach nothing extra. Rejecting VR here is a defensible pedagogical choice, and slide 4 will say so out loud.

The three registers, and why there are exactly three

A learner has to feel a consequence, then measure it, then locate it among alternatives. Drop any one and the lesson fails: feeling alone is a cartoon, measurement alone is a spreadsheet, and comparison alone is somebody else's conclusion.

Design rule

Controls appear when the stage needs them, not before. Stage 1 shows four sliders and nothing else — no controller list, no toggles, no map legend. A learner given every option at once explores none of them. By stage 5 the full console is live and it feels earned rather than dumped.

Section 05How they play

The core verb is dispatch. One question, asked 288 times: how much power at each of four stations, right now? Everything else in the interface exists to help the learner answer that question better than they did last time.

Input — three paths, one state

PathPrimary forWhat it feels like
Mouse / trackpadEveryone, by defaultGrab a handle, drag it, watch the village answer
KeyboardRepeat users, accessibility, and filming the videoNumeric, fast, exactly repeatable — the same run twice
TouchPhone and tabletSame handles, larger targets, map collapses to a summary

All three drive one state object. There is no "mouse mode" — a learner can drag a slider, then nudge it with an arrow key, then scrub with a finger, without ever changing modes.

No gamepad, no VR controller, no headset

The skill being taught is judgement under a physical constraint, not manual dexterity. Every input device we add is a competence the learner must acquire that has nothing to do with power systems, and every one of them narrows who can take part. A mouse and a keyboard are already on every machine that will ever run this.

The five interactions worth remembering

These are the moments the video is built around, and the reason this is a simulator rather than an animated slide deck.

  1. The two-handle slider — you feel the constraint push back

    Each station slider carries two handles: the raw request you are dragging, and the projected command the safety layer will actually execute. Drag the raw handle and the safe one follows it — until it doesn't. Push past the point where the voltage band binds and the safe handle stops moving while yours keeps going, and the gap between them fills with red.

    At station 1 on the weak grid, dragging to −80 kW leaves the safe handle stuck at −54.13 kW with a 25.87 kW gap. Switch to the strong grid with G and the same drag goes all the way to −80.00. Table 5.3, as a hand movement, in about four seconds.

  2. Drag the village itself

    Grab a charger in the isometric scene and pull it up or down. Identical command, embodied in the place it happens. Deliberately imprecise — this is for feeling out how the feeder responds, not for setting a number. When precision matters the learner moves to the slider on their own.

  3. Scrub and branch — the counterfactual machine

    Drag the clock backwards to any of the 288 steps and act differently from there. Your original run stays on screen as a faded ghost, and both outcomes land as dots on the map.

    Reality gives you exactly one attempt from any given state. This gives unlimited attempts from identical states — which is the only way anyone ever learns which of their decisions actually mattered.

  4. The ghost trace — race a controller in real time

    A dotted line on the voltage chart shows where Shielded RL would have put the feeder at this exact step, while you are still driving. You see the gap between your judgement and the trained controller's continuously, without leaving your own run and without being told the answer.

  5. Pull the voltage — sensitivity taught by hand (stretch)

    Grab the voltage curve at bus 18 and drag it upward. The system inverse-solves for the station command that would produce it and moves the slider to match: "to lift bus 18 by 0.01 pu you need 110 kW at station 1 — and 4 kW at station 2 does nothing at all."

    This is ∂V/∂P delivered as a physical sensation rather than a partial derivative. It is on the stretch tier because it needs a stable inverse, but it is the single most original interaction in the build.

The honest comparison: hardware-in-the-loop

The nearest real equivalent is a HIL rig — RTDS, Typhoon HIL, OPAL-RT. It is worth being precise about what it does and does not do, because a judge may know this equipment and a hand-wave would cost more than it gains.

A HIL rig genuinely covers rows 1, 5 and 8, and it does something we cannot: real electromagnetic transients against real hardware in real time. Our claim is not that we replace it. But it costs tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars, occupies one room, seats two or three students, needs a trained operator, and has to be booked. Bus 18 runs on a phone, for free, anywhere, forever, with no supervisor — and it still cannot do rows 4, 7 or 9, because no physical apparatus can.

And then the one that decides it: the misconception at the centre of this entry cannot be taught by real experience at all. An operator who deploys the "safest" controller and serves nobody is handed a report saying the controller is performing perfectly — violations at an all-time low, costs negative. The failure is invisible without the counterfactual, and reality never supplies the counterfactual. This specific lesson is structurally unteachable outside a simulation. That is the sentence slide 4 is built on.

The one measurement worth designing the study around

At stage 6 the learner is shown a leaderboard containing only violation rate and net cost — the two columns a manager would actually be handed. The strong→weak Plain RL agent tops both: violation 0.015, the best on the board, and net cost −$38.2, the only profitable row. The learner picks a controller to deploy.

Then the third column appears. SoC met: 0.000. Not one vehicle charged, all day. And because your Monte-Carlo check confirmed that 100% of energy-service requests were physically deliverable at this operating point, the failure is unambiguously the controller's, not the demand's.

We record what fraction of testers picked it. If 7 of 9 engineering students choose the AI that serves nobody, that single number is stronger evidence than any pre/post delta — it demonstrates the misconception exists in exactly the population being taught. MedXperience took second place last year on n = 9. Almost nobody brings numbers at all.

Section 09The six stages

Roughly 22 minutes end to end — the length that fits a lab session and a tester's patience. Each stage has an entry condition, a single idea, and a reveal.

1
You drive · 4 min

Fail first, before you meet any solution

Manual dispatch of four stations across a 288-step day. No controller menu, no map legend, no advice. You will overcharge and darken the feeder end, or you will play safe and send everyone home with an empty battery. Almost nobody threads it.

Your dot lands on the Map before you have seen a single controller. This ordering is load-bearing and non-negotiable: you cannot be impressed by a solution to a problem you have not personally felt.

2
Uncoordinated · 2 min

Service without safety

Everybody charges the instant they plug in. SoC met 0.996 — near-perfect service. Violation rate 0.116, and the village goes dark down the whole tail at the evening peak, exactly when EV arrivals collide with the disappearance of PV.

Reveal: the second dot on the Map, far right and near the top. The shape of the trade-off starts to exist.

3
Droop, IEEE 1547 · 3 min

Safety without service

A fixed rule from the standard. Violations fall to 0.052, the best of any method in the study; the lights hold. SoC met collapses to 0.325 — the cars go home empty.

The margin slider unlocks here. Drag it and watch safety and service trade against each other in real time. Conservatism stops being a virtue and becomes a price.

4
Plain deep RL · 4 min

The credibility beat — the AI loses

Plain RL, a fully-trained deep reinforcement learning agent: violations 0.090, SoC met 0.277. Its dot lands below and to the right of the droop dot.

Strictly dominated by a rule from the 1970s. More violations and less service. The learner is told, plainly, that the obvious application of deep RL to this problem does not work.

This is the stage that buys the entry its credibility. A demo where the author's method wins every chart is a sales pitch. A demo that shows the fashionable technique failing first has earned the right to the next stage.

5
Physics restored · 5 min

The same agent, with the network put back in

Shielded RL: same SAC backbone, same training budget, plus a safety projection rebuilt every control step from voltage sensitivities measured on the live network. Service 0.277 → 0.569 at statistically identical safety (p = 0.86, Cohen's d = 2.04 on the service gain).

The projection was in the loop while Shielded RL was trained. That is what makes this a different policy rather than the same policy behind a filter — and it is why the comparison that matters is Shielded RL against Plain RL, not Shielded RL against itself.

The toggle shows the filter working, not a collapse. Turn the projection off and Shielded RL's raw commands are executed unfiltered: the station-1 handle jumps from −54.13 kW to the full −80, the voltage profile drops through the red line, and the learner sees exactly how often and how hard the safety layer had been intervening all along. The permanent record of what it bought is on the Map — two dots, Plain RL and Shielded RL, at the same safety and twice the service.

The station-1 readout does the rest: raw −80 kW in, −54.13 kW out on the weak grid, −80.00 kW out on the strong. Same policy, same request, different physics, different command.

6
The trap · 4 min

Best safety score, zero cars charged

A leaderboard with two columns: violation rate and net cost. Strong→weak Plain RL leads both — 0.015 violations, −$38.2 cost. "Deploy it."

The third column arrives. SoC met: 0.000 ± 0.000. Then the replay: the agent's set-points, flat at zero, all day. It satisfied its constraint by refusing to do its job.

Debrief: the eight objectives, the learner's own Map with every dot they generated, their pick against the cohort's, and a one-page export they can keep.

What actually runs live

Two routines. That is the whole runtime.

  1. The voltage model. v = v₀ + Sᴾ·p + Sᴼ·q — a 33×4 matrix–vector product, about five lines of TypeScript, well under a tenth of a millisecond. This is the same linearised model your safety layer uses, so the simulator and the reference study agree by construction.
  2. The safety projection. Because the voltage model is linear in the command u, the feasible set {u : 0.95 ≤ v₀ + S·u ≤ 1.05, u_min ≤ u ≤ u_max} is a polytope. Projecting onto a polytope does not need a conic solver — Dykstra's alternating projection converges in sub-millisecond time for 4 variables and ~70 constraints. Same constraints, same 0.010 pu margin, same answer as your 8-variable SOCP.

Everything else — the six controllers, the load and PV profiles, the EV arrivals, the price schedule — is array playback. Reading numbers you already computed. This is also why the demo physically cannot break mid-recording: there is no solver to fail, no server to time out, no Python in the path.
