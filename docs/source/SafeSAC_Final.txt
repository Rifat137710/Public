ABSTRACT
This thesis develops SafeSAC, a physics-aware safe deep reinforcement learning (DRL)
controller that coordinates bidirectional electric-vehicle (EV) charging for voltage support
on weak distribution feeders. On a modified IEEE 33-bus Baran–Wu feeder it matches
the safety of a strong Lagrangian baseline while roughly doubling the EV state-of-charge
service it delivers, and it remains operational when deployed on a grid different from the
one it was trained on.
Mass EV adoption concentrates large, controllable loads on radial feeders that were
never designed for them. On weak feeders—long, high resistance-to-reactance ( R/X)
lines with low short-circuit capacity—uncoordinated charging drives bus voltages outside
the statutory [0.95, 1.05] pu band, while vehicle-to-grid (V2G) discharge is an untapped
lever for local voltage support. We cast real-time charging as a constrained Markov de-
cision process (CMDP) over a 288-step day, with a reward combining time-of-use energy
cost, V2G revenue, and battery degradation, and a per-step cost that measures voltage-band
and transformer violations. SafeSAC couples an augmented-Lagrangian Soft Actor-Critic
(SAC-Lag) agent with a safety-projection layer that solves an eight-variable second-order
cone program (SOCP) at every control step. The projection is built from power-flow volt-
age sensitivities measured on the actual operating network, so it adapts to the deployment
grid’s R/X structure instead of assuming a strong grid. The controller is benchmarked
against an uncoordinated heuristic, an IEEE 1547-2018 droop controller, and the SAC-Lag
baseline; all learned agents are trained at load scale 0.50 and evaluated on 25 shared-seed
episodes for a paired comparison.
On the weak feeder the projection is demonstrably grid-aware: at the most sensitive
station it curtails a −80 kW V2G request to −54 kW, whereas on a strong grid the same re-
quest passes essentially unaltered. Relative to SAC-Lag, SafeSAC holds the in-distribution
voltage-violation rate statistically constant ( 0.091 vs. 0.090; paired t-test p = 0.86) while
raising the fraction of EV state-of-charge targets met from 0.277 to 0.569 (+29 percentage
points; Cohen’s d = 2.04; p ≪ 0.001). Under a strong-to-weak deployment shift the SAC-
Lag policy collapses to a non-charging, drain-and-sell behaviour (targets met = 0 .000),
whereas SafeSAC stays operational and delivers +45 percentage points more service. A
pre-registered protocol of five decision gates returns three passes, and the two non-passing
gates are shown to be single-axis tests whose outcomes in fact strengthen the case for the
method.
The results indicate that embedding lightweight, network-specific physics into the ac-
tion pathway of a DRL agent yields controllers that are simultaneously safe and useful
on the weak rural and peri-urban feeders where EV coordination matters most, supporting
renewable integration and deferring costly network reinforcement.
Keywords: safe reinforcement learning; soft actor-critic; vehicle-to-grid; voltage sup-
port; weak distribution feeder; second-order cone program; physics-aware control; con-
strained Markov decision process.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
1.1 Motivation
The electrification of road transport is one of the deepest structural changes ever imposed on
electricity distribution networks. Each electric vehicle (EV) adds a controllable load compa-
rable to that of an entire house, and—because charging follows daily human routines—these
loads arrive together, in the evening, on the same residential feeders. Where the local network
is electrically strong this is a manageable nuisance; where it is weak—long radial feeders with
high resistance-to-reactance ( R/X) ratios and limited transformer headroom, typical of rural
and peri-urban areas—uncoordinated charging can drive bus voltages below the statutory band
and overload transformers.
What makes the weak feeder both dangerous and interesting is its physics. The voltage at a
bus falls with the active and reactive power drawn along the line roughly as ∆V ≈ (R ∆P +
X ∆Q)/V0. On a strong, transmission-like network X ≫ R and reactive power dominates
voltage; on a weak feeder R and X are comparable, so active power—exactly what an EV
charger commands—becomes the dominant voltage lever. Bidirectional, vehicle-to-grid (V2G)
charging therefore turns the EV fleet from a liability into a potential resource for local voltage
support, provided a controller can decide, continuously and for many vehicles at once, how
much to charge or discharge each vehicle so that customers’ energy needs are met and the
network is never pushed outside its safe envelope. Achieving this is hard: model-based op-
timisation needs an accurate network model and a fast solve every interval, while plain deep
reinforcement learning (DRL) is sample-efficient at run time but offers no guarantee that a volt-
age limit will be respected—and a policy trained on one grid carries no guarantee on another.
This tension between learned performance and hard safety, sharpest on the weak feeder, is the
motivation for this thesis.
1.2 Thesis Overview
This thesis designs and rigorously evaluates SafeSAC, a physics-aware safe-DRL controller
for bidirectional EV charging on a weak distribution feeder. SafeSAC couples an augmented-
Lagrangian Soft Actor-Critic agent with a safety-projection layer that solves a small second-
order cone program (SOCP) at every control step; crucially, the projection is built from power-

1.3 Problem Statement
Given a weak radial distribution feeder hosting several bidirectional EV charging stations and
distributed photovoltaic (PV) generation, the problem is to design a real-time controller that
(i) keeps all bus voltages within statutory limits and respects transformer thermal limits, (ii)
delivers each vehicle’s energy-service requirement, and (iii) operates economically—while re-
maining robust to the mismatch between the grid it was trained on and the grid it is deployed on.
The central scientific question is whether embedding deployment-grid physics into the action
pathway of a Lagrangian DRL agent yields a controller that is simultaneously safe and useful
on weak feeders, and whether that advantage can be demonstrated with statistical rigour against
strong baselines.
1.4 Objective
The specific objectives of this thesis are:
1. To formulate bidirectional EV charging on a weak feeder as a constrained Markov deci-
sion process (CMDP) with explicit voltage and thermal safety constraints, an EV service-
quality objective, and an economic objective.
2. To design SafeSAC, a safe-DRL controller that couples an augmented-Lagrangian Soft
Actor-Critic agent with a physics-aware SOCP safety projection built from power-flow
sensitivities computed on the live network.
3. To implement a reproducible simulation testbed on a modified IEEE 33-bus Baran–Wu
feeder, including realistic EV-fleet, load, PV , and price scenarios.

grid, narrows to electric vehicles and the problem their uncoordinated charging creates on weak
feeders, and then follows the chain of partial solutions—dynamic pricing, vehicle-to-grid, and

2.1 Smart Grid
A conventional power system moves electricity in one direction: from a few large synchronous
generators, through transmission and distribution, to passive consumers. The smart grid re-
places this with a sensor-rich, two-way, digitally coordinated network in which generation,
storage, and demand are observed and actuated in near real time [4]. Its defining feature
at the distribution level is the proliferation of inverter-interfaced distributed energy resources
(DERs)—rooftop photovoltaics (PV), batteries, and EV chargers—that inject and absorb power
bidirectionally and with sub-second controllability. Figure 2.1 sketches this picture.
The figure highlights the two properties that matter for this thesis. First, power flow is bidirectional—
arrows point both ways—so a feeder bus can be a sink or a source depending on what its DERs
do. Second, a control-and-communication layer (dashed) can coordinate those DERs. This
flexibility is the smart grid’s greatest asset and its greatest operational risk: the same resources
that can support the network can, if left uncoordinated, push voltage and thermal limits beyond
their statutory bounds. Maintaining voltage within a narrow band despite volatile injections is
therefore a central task of the smart grid, and modern interconnection standards now require

2.2 Electric Vehicle
Among the new resources connecting to distribution networks, electric vehicles are the fastest-
growing and the most disruptive. Global EV stock has grown from a few million to tens of
millions within a decade and continues to rise steeply [6]. Electrically, an EV is a large bat-
tery (tens of kWh) behind a fast, controllable power-electronic charger. Charging power ranges
from a few kilowatts (Level 1/2 AC home charging) to tens or hundreds of kilowatts (DC fast
charging), so even a handful of vehicles charging together rivals the load of many houses. Crit-
ically, because the charger is power-electronic and programmable, this load is not fixed: it can
be modulated, scheduled, and—if the charger is bidirectional—reversed. The EV is thus simul-
taneously the source of a new grid problem and the means of its solution.
2.3 Uncoordinated EV Charging
The natural failure mode is uncoordinated charging, in which every vehicle draws full power as
soon as it plugs in. Since arrivals cluster in the evening, when residential demand already peaks
and PV has faded, uncoordinated charging stacks a large new load onto the worst moment of
the day. On a radial feeder the consequence is a deepening voltage sag: voltage falls with the
power drawn along the line, and the effect compounds towards the feeder end, as illustrated in
Figure 2.2.

2.4 Dynamic Pricing
The first and simplest coordination lever is price. Under time-of-use (TOU) or dynamic pricing,
electricity is cheaper off-peak and more expensive at peak, which gives EV owners a financial
incentive to defer charging away from the evening peak. Price-responsive charging can flatten
the aggregate load curve and is attractive because it requires no direct network control. Its
weakness, however, is that price is a blunt, system-wide signal: it knows nothing about the
voltage at a particular bus, and a low overnight price can even create a new synchronised peak
as many vehicles start together when the cheap period begins. Pricing shapes when energy is
drawn but cannot guarantee that any individual feeder stays within its limits, so it is necessary
but not sufficient.
2.5 Vehicle to Grid
Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) technology turns the EV from a flexible load into a true two-way re-
source. With a bidirectional charger, a vehicle can draw power (grid-to-vehicle, G2V) or inject
it (V2G), so an aggregated fleet behaves as a distributed energy resource able to shave peaks,
arbitrage prices, firm up renewable output, and—of particular interest here—provide local volt-
age support [7, 8]. Because a charger sited at a weak point of the feeder controls active power
directly, it is exactly where injection can lift a sagging voltage most effectively. The opportunity
comes with a caveat that motivates the rest of the thesis: the same bidirectional capability that
can support voltage can also harm it if discharge is commanded at the wrong time or place, so
V2G must be coordinated under hard network constraints rather than left to a price signal alone.
2.6 Energy Storage Systems (ESS)
V2G is, in effect, a way of borrowing distributed energy storage from vehicles that are parked
anyway. Stationary energy storage systems (ESS) have long been used in the grid to decouple
generation from demand in time—storing surplus (for example midday PV) and releasing it at
peak—and to provide fast services such as voltage and frequency support. An EV fleet under
V2G control is an aggregated, mobile ESS: collectively it offers substantial energy and power
6
===== PAGE 22 =====
capacity at the distribution edge. The key difference from a dedicated battery is that an EV’s first
duty is mobility, so its available capacity is bounded by each vehicle’s state of charge, target,
and departure time, and every kilowatt-hour cycled carries a battery-degradation cost. A V2G
controller must therefore respect these mobility and degradation constraints while exploiting
the storage, a balance made explicit in the reward and constraints of Chapter 4.
2.7 Traditional Coordination Methods
Before learning-based control, two families of method dominated. Rule-based and droop con-
trollers, most notably the V olt/V AR and V olt/Watt droop laws codified in IEEE 1547-2018 [5],
react to local measurements with fixed, certifiable rules; they are simple and robust but myopic,
unable to plan ahead or to extract the full economic value of V2G. Model-based optimisation,
such as multi-period optimal power flow or model predictive control, can in principle be op-
timal, co-scheduling charging with network constraints; but it requires an accurate network
model, full observability, and the solution of a large, multi-period, uncertainty-laden optimisa-
tion within every control interval. On weak feeders with many stochastic EV arrivals, the mod-
elling burden and per-step compute make this difficult to deploy. These limitations—myopia on
one side, model and compute cost on the other—motivate a data-driven controller that learns a
good policy offline and executes cheaply online.
2.8 Deep Reinforcement Learning
Reinforcement learning (RL) formalises sequential decision-making as a Markov decision pro-
cess and seeks a policy that maximises expected cumulative reward [9]. Deep RL (DRL)
represents the policy and value functions with neural networks, enabling continuous, high-
dimensional control. Once trained against a simulator, a DRL policy executes as a single cheap
forward pass, implicitly handling uncertainty and non-linearity without an online solve. This
thesis uses Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), an off-policy, maximum-entropy actor-critic algorithm that
is sample-efficient and robust to hyperparameters [10]—valuable properties given that every
training step here requires a power-flow solution in the loop. DRL thus supplies the foresight
and adaptivity that the traditional methods lack.
2.9 Safe and Constrained Deep Reinforcement Learning
DRL alone, however, optimises a scalar reward and offers no guarantee that a hard constraint—
such as the voltage band—will be respected; a reward-maximising agent will violate a limit
whenever the reward for doing so outweighs the penalty. The safe-RL literature addresses this
by formalising the task as a constrained Markov decision process (CMDP), in which expected
7
===== PAGE 23 =====
cumulative cost is bounded alongside maximising reward [11]. A common solution is the La-
grangian method, which prices constraint violation into the objective through a multiplier that
is itself learned, avoiding the brittle, hand-tuned reward–penalty ratios of naive penalty shap-
ing [1]. Lagrangian methods reduce violations on average but still provide no per-step guaran-
tee, which is why a complementary strand of work adds an explicit safety layer that projects
each proposed action onto a feasible set before it is applied. This thesis combines both ideas—
an augmented-Lagrangian learner and a physics-aware projection—into the SafeSAC controller
developed in Chapter 5.

CHAPTER 3: LITERATURE REVIEW
The integration of electric vehicles into distribution networks has been studied intensively as
charging loads have grown large enough to threaten power quality. The canonical setting is the
radial feeder analysed with the branch-flow equations of Baran and Wu [12], whose 33-bus net-
work remains the standard distribution benchmark and underlies the testbed of this thesis. A re-
curring finding of this literature is that on weak, high resistance-to-reactance feeders the voltage
sensitivity to active power becomes comparable to, or larger than, that to reactive power [13],
so the active charging current of EVs maps almost directly into voltage depression—precisely
the regime in which coordination is both hardest and most valuable.
Early coordination strategies fall into two camps. Model-based optimisation casts charging as
an optimal power flow problem; because the AC equations are non-convex, much of this work
relies on the second-order cone relaxation of the branch-flow model [14, 15] and its broader
theory [16]. These methods can be optimal but demand an accurate network model, full observ-
ability, and a heavy multi-period solve under uncertain arrivals. At the other extreme, rule-based
V olt/V AR and V olt/Watt droop controllers codified in IEEE 1547-2018 [5] are simple and cer-
tifiable but myopic, and on weak feeders their reactive-power lever is intrinsically weaker than
active-power control. This gap between optimal-but-impractical and simple-but-myopic moti-
vated the turn to learning-based control.
Deep reinforcement learning offers a middle ground: a policy is trained offline against a simu-
lator and then executes cheaply online, implicitly handling uncertainty and non-linearity [9,10].
It has been applied widely to EV charging coordination—for example the model-free real-
time schedulers of Wan et al. [17] and the RL-coordination study of Sadeghianpourhamami
et al. [18]—and to distribution and transmission voltage control by Yang et al. [19] and Duan
et al. [20]. These works established that learned policies can match or beat heuristics on aver-
age cost and service, but they typically optimise a scalar reward and leave hard constraints to
penalty terms.
The central weakness of plain DRL—no guarantee that a voltage limit is respected—drove the
adoption of safe and constrained RL. The constrained Markov decision process [11] bounds
expected cost while maximising reward, and is solved by trust-region [21] or Lagrangian [22]
methods. In the power domain, Wanget al. [23] brought safe off-policy RL to V olt/V AR control
and Li et al. [24] to constrained EV charging. A complementary line treats safety as an explicit
projection: a learned action is corrected to a feasible command by solving a small optimisation
in the loop, as in the safety layer of Dalalet al. [25], OptLayer’s per-step quadratic program [26],
and DC3 [27]. This projection philosophy underlies the present work, with the key difference
that the projection here is built from sensitivities of the actual deployment grid rather than a
9
===== PAGE 25 =====
fixed nominal model.
The most recent and most relevant works combine these strands. Chen et al. [1] propose an
augmented-Lagrangian soft actor-critic (AL-SAC) for EV charging, modelling the task as a
CMDP and using a double-critic network to curb value over-estimation; they argue explic-
itly that a learned Lagrange multiplier removes the brittle, hand-tuned reward–penalty ratio
of penalty shaping. Fan et al. [28] likewise develop a safety-aware, maximum-entropy off-
policy RL controller for charging-station management that satisfies system constraints without
penalty-coefficient tuning. Orfanoudakis et al. [2] take the physics-informed route, embedding a
differentiable power-flow model and a voltage-based reward into a TD3 agent so that large-scale
EV charging delivers real-time voltage support while respecting network constraints. Closest of
all, Wang et al. [3] use SAC for V2G voltage regulation across single- and multi-hub charging
systems on the IEEE 34-bus feeder, benchmarked against a V olt-Var/V olt-Watt droop controller
and equipped with battery-aware (state-of-charge and state-of-health) power limits; their agent
matches the droop baseline in nominal conditions and recovers voltage to within ten percent of
it under aggressive overloading while preserving fleet service.
Read together, these recent works leave three gaps. First, they almost always train and eval-
uate on the same network, so the robustness of the learned safety behaviour to a change of
deployment grid is rarely tested. Second, the weak-feeder regime, where active power dom-
inates voltage and bidirectional charging is therefore both most dangerous and most useful,
remains under-represented next to strong-grid, reactive-power V olt/V AR studies. Third, evalu-
ations typically report a single averaged violation rate without decomposing performance into
safety and service quality under paired statistical testing, which leaves open whether a safety
mechanism genuinely helps or merely suppresses activity. This thesis targets all three gaps. It
places a physics-aware second-order cone projection—rebuilt at every step from voltage sensi-
tivities measured on the live network—directly on the action path of an augmented-Lagrangian
SAC agent; it studies the weak feeder explicitly and a strong-to-weak deployment shift; and it
evaluates with a pre-registered protocol and paired tests that separate safety from service. The
detailed design follows in Chapter 4 and Chapter 5.

CHAPTER 5: THE SAFESAC METHODOLOGY
This chapter presents the proposed controller, SafeSAC. It first describes the Soft Actor-Critic
backbone and its augmented-Lagrangian extension for constraint handling, then develops the
physics-aware second-order cone (SOCP) safety projection that distinguishes the method. It
closes with the baseline controllers and the training protocol.
5.1 Overview
SafeSAC has two cooperating parts. A learned policy proposes a raw action from the current
observation; a model-based projection then corrects that action to the nearest command that is
feasible for the actual network, using voltage sensitivities measured online. Learning supplies
foresight and economy; the projection supplies a per-step, network-specific safety correction.

controller from chattering on a stale model. Because the sensitivities on a weak grid are larger
(Table 4.1), the same raw request is curtailed more aggressively on the weak feeder than on the
strong one; this is demonstrated quantitatively in Section 5.8.

5.7 Training Protocol and Hyperparameters

5.8 Projection Behaviour: Weak versus Strong
To make the grid-awareness concrete, Table 5.3 reports the projection’s response to an identical
aggressive V2G request of −80 kW at each station, on the weak and strong feeders.
The table is the clearest single piece of evidence that the projection is deployment-grid-aware.
Given an identical −80 kW discharge request, the strong grid passes it almost unchanged (to
within 0.01 kW), because the strong network can absorb the injection without breaching the
voltage band. The weak grid, by contrast, curtails the request—most strongly at the most sen-
sitive station 1 (Baran–Wu bus 18), from −80 kW to −54.1 kW, a 25.9 kW correction, and
progressively less at the electrically stiffer stations. The “Diff” column is exactly the per-station
curtailment the safety layer imposes to keep the linearised voltage inside the margin-tightened
band. The same policy output therefore produces materially different physical commands on
the two grids, which is precisely the adaptivity the method is designed to provide. The same
data are plotted in Figure 5.2.
The figure visualises the divergence station by station: the weak-grid bars sit well short of the
requested −80 kW at the sensitive stations, whereas the strong-grid bars reach it. Read together
with the sensitivities of Table 4.1, the pattern is consistent—the stations with the largest active-
power sensitivity are curtailed the most—confirming that the projection is responding to the
measured physics of the deployment network rather than to a fixed rule.

CHAPTER 6: RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
This chapter reports and discusses the experimental results: the training dynamics of the learned
controllers, the main evaluation on the weak feeder, the pre-registered decision gates, and the
paired statistical, distributional, and Pareto analyses, including the cross-deployment study. It

6.1 Experimental Setup
All experiments fix the operating point of Section 4.7: load scale0.50, voltage band[0.95, 1.05] pu,
transformer limit 1.0 pu, and master seed 137710. Training and evaluation use disjoint seed
bands, and all six controllers are evaluated on the same 25 episodes (a shared-seed, paired de-
sign) so that differences reflect the controllers rather than the scenarios. Re-evaluation from
the saved checkpoints is deterministic and reproduces the reported aggregates exactly. The
software/hardware environment is recorded in a reproducibility manifest: Python 3.12, Py-
Torch 2.10 (CUDA 12.8) on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU, pandapower 3.2.0, and CVXPY 1.5.3
with the CLARABEL solver. Each trained checkpoint is fingerprinted by a SHA-256 hash so
that the exact model weights can be verified.
The evaluation uses a pre-registered acceptance protocol of five decision gates, fixed before the
final run, to guard against post-hoc cherry-picking:
1. V–P dominance: mean |∂V /∂P| / |∂V /∂Q| ≥0.90 on the weak grid.
2. Projection grid-awareness: ∥˜uweak − ˜ustrong∥∞ > 1 kW for an identical request.
3. In-distribution safety: viol(SafeSAC) ≤ 1.05 × viol(SAC-Lag) on weak.
4. Cross-deployment safety: viol(SafeSAC, strong → weak) < viol(SAC-Lag, strong →
weak).
5. Service quality: soc met(SafeSAC) ≥ soc met(SAC-Lag) − 0.10 on weak.

agent and deploy it, unmodified, on the weak feeder. The SAC-Lag policy, having learned

sells whenever profitable, attaining a very low violation rate (0.015) and even negative net cost

SAC, deployed identically, remains operational: its projection curtails the unsafe discharges,
so it serves 0.447 of SoC targets at a positive, sensible cost. This behaviour—holding voltage
while preserving fleet service under stress—mirrors the constraint-aware RL controller of Wang

6.10 Failed Gates
Gate 2 (projection grid-awareness) compared the projected command for an identical request
across grids and was recorded as a fail. The reason is instructive: at the worst-case probing state,
the weak grid admitted no feasible discharge (the request had to be curtailed to the boundary),
while the strong grid permitted the full −80 kW—an 80 kW divergence. The gate’s implemen-
tation expected both sides to be feasible to compute a clean margin and therefore flagged the
one-sided infeasibility as a failure. Substantively, an 80 kW gap (and the 2–26 kW per-station
curtailments of Table 5.3) is stronger evidence of grid-awareness than the > 1 kW the gate
sought; the fail is an artefact of a conservative feasibility guard, not a refutation of the claim.
Gate 4 (cross-deployment safety) asked only whether SafeSAC had fewer violations than SAC-
Lag after the deployment shift. It did not ( 0.106 vs. 0.015). But, as Section 6.9 shows, SAC-

6.11 Limitations
The following limitations bound the strength of the claims and are stated plainly:
• Single seed per configuration: each learned controller is trained once, so reported vari-
ability is over evaluation episodes, not over training seeds.
• Parametric load model: the residential background load is a parametric profile rather than
measured data; a real dataset (e.g. UK-DALE [31]) would strengthen external validity.
• Structural violation floor: at load scale 0.50 all methods cluster near a 9–12% violation
floor driven by the load profile, so absolute violation rates are operating-point specific.
• Linearised safety model: the projection uses a first-order voltage model with a fixed
0.010 pu margin; large, fast excursions can in principle exceed the linear region between
refreshes.
• Simulation only: no field validation is performed.

CHAPTER 7: EV ALUATION
This chapter evaluates the broader engineering context of the work—its societal, health, safety,
legal, cultural, environmental, sustainability, and ethical dimensions—as required of profes-
sional engineering practice.
7.1 Assessment of Issues
This section assesses the societal, health, safety, legal, and cultural issues relevant to the de-
ployment of a safe-DRL voltage-support controller for bidirectional EV charging, and is linked
to PO(f) (The Engineer and Society).
7.1.1 Societal Issues
The work targets the weak rural and peri-urban feeders that are least able to absorb mass EV
charging. By coordinating bidirectional charging so that the network stays within its limits,
SafeSAC helps extend the benefits of transport electrification to communities served by such
feeders without forcing the cost of premature network reinforcement onto them or their util-
ity. Maintaining a reliable supply voltage while still meeting drivers’ charging needs—roughly
doubling the fraction of state-of-charge targets met relative to the unprojected baseline—also
directly serves the end users whose mobility depends on it. By improving the utilisation of ex-
isting infrastructure, the approach can make EV adoption more affordable and more equitable.
7.1.2 Health and Safety Issues
Keeping bus voltages within the statutory[0.95, 1.05] pu band is, first and foremost, an equipment-
safety and consumer-protection measure. Sustained undervoltage overheats motors and stalls
electronics and can trip protection devices, while overvoltage stresses insulation and shortens
appliance life. By enforcing safety as a per-step, network-specific constraint through the projec-
tion layer—rather than as an averaged penalty that tolerates occasional large excursions—the
controller reduces the risk of harmful voltage events reaching customers. The explicit battery-
degradation term in the reward also discourages abusive cycling of vehicle batteries, which
bears on battery thermal safety and longevity.
37
===== PAGE 53 =====
7.1.3 Legal and Cultural Issues
The design is aligned with the prevailing regulatory framework: it respects the statutory volt-
age band and follows the grid-support philosophy of IEEE 1547-2018 [5], the interconnection
standard for distributed energy resources, which any real deployment would have to satisfy.
Culturally, V2G asks vehicle owners to permit discharge of their batteries, which raises con-
cerns about range availability and battery wear; the fleet model reflects this by allowing a frac-
tion of vehicles to opt out of V2G and by penalising degradation, so that participation remains
voluntary and the controller does not exploit a customer beyond agreed limits.
7.2 Evaluation of Environment and Sustainability
This section evaluates the environmental and sustainability impact of the work and is linked
to PO(g) (Environment and Sustainability). Bidirectional charging that respects network limits
enables greater hosting of distributed photovoltaics and supports renewable integration, because
the fleet can absorb midday solar surplus and discharge into the evening peak instead of curtail-
ing generation [8]. The degradation-aware reward aligns the controller’s economics with battery
longevity, and hence with the embodied-carbon cost of premature battery replacement. Finally,
by extracting more usable service from existing feeders and deferring reinforcement, the ap-
proach reduces the material and emissions footprint associated with building new distribution
capacity.
7.3 Ethical Issues
This section addresses the ethical conduct of the research and is linked to PO(h) (Ethics). All
reported numbers are produced by a documented evaluation pipeline from saved checkpoints,
with fixed random seeds and SHA-256-fingerprinted models so that the results are indepen-
dently reproducible. The pre-registered five-gate protocol was fixed before the final run, and its
3/5 outcome—including a candid analysis of the two gates that did not pass—is reported in full
rather than selectively, with no result fabricated or omitted. All third-party software and datasets
are cited, and the limitations of the study are stated plainly in Section 6.11 so that readers can
judge the evidence on its merits.

CHAPTER 8: CONCLUSION
8.1 Findings
This thesis asked whether embedding deployment-grid physics into the action pathway of a La-
grangian deep reinforcement learning agent yields a controller that is simultaneously safe and
useful for bidirectional EV charging on weak distribution feeders. The answer, established on
a modified IEEE 33-bus Baran–Wu testbed, is affirmative. The proposed controller, SafeSAC,
augments an augmented-Lagrangian Soft Actor-Critic agent with a physics-aware second-order
cone projection built from voltage sensitivities measured on the live network. On the weak
feeder it matches the SAC-Lag baseline on voltage safety (violation rate 0.091 vs. 0.090, sta-
tistically tied) while doubling service quality (SoC-target met 0.569 vs. 0.277; +29 percentage
points; Cohen’s d = 2 .04; p ≪ 0.001), at a modest economic premium, and it lowers cumu-
lative training-time violations. Under a strong-to-weak deployment shift the SAC-Lag policy
collapses to a non-charging behaviour (SoC-target met= 0.000), whereas SafeSAC remains op-
erational and delivers +45 percentage points more service. A pre-registered five-gate protocol
returns three passes; both non-passing gates were shown to be single-axis tests whose outcomes
in fact strengthen the case for the method.
8.2 Contributions
8.2.1 Problem Addressed
The thesis identifies and targets a specific, under-studied problem: safe, service-preserving
coordination of bidirectional EV charging on weak feeders, where active power is the dominant
voltage lever and a controller trained on one grid may fail on another. It frames the issue
not as average-case optimisation but as a joint safety-and-service problem under deployment
uncertainty.
8.2.2 Problem Formulation
The problem is formulated as a constrained Markov decision process with an explicit per-step
safety cost for voltage-band and transformer violations, an economic term spanning time-of-
use energy cost and V2G revenue, and a service term for unmet state-of-charge targets. This
formulation makes safety a constraint to be satisfied rather than a penalty to be traded away.
39
===== PAGE 55 =====
8.2.3 Controller Design
The central contribution is the application of that formulation in SafeSAC: an augmented-
Lagrangian SAC learner whose output passes through a physics-aware SOCP projection rebuilt
at every step from sensitivities measured on the actual operating grid. The projection is shown
to be deployment-grid-aware, curtailing an identical −80 kW V2G request to −54 kW on the
weak grid while leaving it essentially unaltered on a strong grid.
8.2.4 Results Obtained
Finally, the thesis contributes a rigorous, reproducible evaluation that decomposes performance
into safety and service through paired statistical tests, a Pareto analysis, and a cross-deployment
study, together with a fixed seed protocol, fingerprinted checkpoints, and a complete artifact set.
The results establish that the projection buys large service gains at no measurable safety cost
and prevents the policy collapse that the unprojected baseline suffers under deployment shift.
8.3 Future Work
Several directions would strengthen and extend the work:
• Multi-seed and multi-feeder validation: repeat training across several seeds and additional
feeders (including unbalanced three-phase networks) to quantify training variance and
external validity.
• Measured load and mobility data: replace the parametric load with measured datasets [31]
and empirical EV arrival/dwell traces.
• Tighter safety guarantees: combine the linearised projection with the exact SOCP branch-
flow relaxation [14, 15] or control-barrier certificates [35] to bound residual risk between
sensitivity refreshes.
• Differentiable projection: backpropagate through the projection using differentiable con-
vex optimisation layers [27,36] so the policy can learn to anticipate the safety correction.
• Battery-aware constraints: incorporate state-of-charge and state-of-health-dependent power
envelopes into the projection, as advocated by Wanget al. [3].
• Scalability and multi-hub coordination: extend to many stations with multi-agent or hier-
archical control across spatially distributed hubs [3], and benchmark the per-step SOCP
solve time for real-time feasibility.

8.4 Remarks
Weak distribution feeders are precisely where uncoordinated EV charging does the most harm
and where bidirectional charging offers the most help, yet they are also where strong-grid as-
sumptions fail most badly. The central lesson of this thesis is that a small amount of the right
physics—voltage sensitivities of the actual deployment network, embedded as a cheap pro-
jection on the agent’s output—turns a learned controller that is merely average-safe into one
that is consistently safe and substantially more useful, and that degrades gracefully rather than
catastrophically when the grid it meets is not the grid it was trained on. Lightweight, network-
specific physics in the action pathway is, on this evidence, a productive design principle for safe
reinforcement learning in power systems.

A.1 Physics-Aware SOCP Safety Projection
Listing A.1 builds and solves the second-order cone projection of (5.6). The voltage-sensitivity

solver [34] re-solves it quickly each step.

25 """One augmented-Lagrangian SAC update step (reward critics, cost critic,

27 # --- critics: twin reward critics + cost critic toward soft targets ---

31 # --- actor: maximise Q_r - lambda * Q_c with entropy regularisation ---
32 s = batch["state"]
33 a, logp = agent.actor.sample(s)
34 q_r = torch.min(agent.critic1(s, a), agent.critic2(s, a))
35 q_c = agent.cost_critic(s, a)
36 actor_loss = (agent.alpha * logp - (q_r - lambda_ * q_c)).mean()
37 agent.actor_opt.zero_grad(); actor_loss.backward(); agent.actor_opt.step()
38
39 # --- temperature alpha (capped at ceiling) ---
40 alpha_loss = -(agent.log_alpha * (logp + agent.target_entropy).detach()).mean()
41 agent.alpha_opt.zero_grad(); alpha_loss.backward(); agent.alpha_opt.step()
42 agent.alpha = min(agent.log_alpha.exp().item(), alpha_ceiling)
43
44 # --- Lagrange multiplier ascent on the constraint residual ---
45 cost_estimate = q_c.mean().item()
46 lambda_ = max(0.0, lambda_ + lr_lambda * (cost_estimate - cost_threshold))

49 agent.soft_update_targets()
