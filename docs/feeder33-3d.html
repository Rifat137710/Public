Bus 18 · design study · IEEE 33-bus feeder

Feeder 33: the town on one wire

This is what the 3D world would look like. Not a sketch of one — the geometry is the feeder's own topology, and every lamp in it is dimmed by a voltage that came out of the same solver the app ships with. Drag to orbit. Pick a controller and watch the far end of the town go dark.

Where the numbers come from. Bus positions are generated by walking the feeder tree — distance across the ground is electrical distance from the substation. Voltages, branch currents, plug-in counts and station commands are captured frame by frame from real episodes on the weak feeder (load 0.50, seed 137710), 144 frames per simulated day. The uncoordinated and droop runs use production controllers; the two learned agents are labelled stand-ins until the trained policies are exported, and they say so on screen.

Streetlights

Brightness is the bus voltage. A healthy lamp is warm amber; as the bus sags the lamp both dims and reddens, exactly as a real one does. Below 0.95 pu it becomes unsteady.

Red conductors

Heat in the line, proportional to I²R. When the town charges all at once the trunk out of the substation visibly glows — that glow is the wasted energy the scorecard counts.

Buildings

Sized by the real load at that bus. Buses 24 and 25 carry 420 kW each — the cold store and the factory. You can read where the demand is without a legend.

Charging stations

Four of them, at buses 18, 22, 25 and 33 — each at the end of a run, which is the worst possible place and exactly why this is hard. Cars appear as they plug in.

The one idea this buys

Everything else in a 3D build is production value. This part is pedagogy.

The reason to build it

Electrical distance becomes walking distance

On a radial feeder, voltage falls monotonically as you move away from the substation. In a diagram that is a line on a chart you have to be taught to read. Here you walk out of town and the lights get worse with every pole. Nobody who has done that needs the voltage profile explained afterwards.

Stage 5 · the hard one

The sensitivity field, made visible

The matrix answers "if I inject 10 kW here, what moves everywhere?" In 3D that is a field: point at a bus, and the whole town lights up with how much that one bus moves each other bus. Then the safe envelope is a volume, and the agent's raw command is a vector you watch bend back inside it.

Stage 6 · the trap

Standing in the town you emptied

You pick a controller from two columns on a clipboard. Then the world runs forward under your choice — and you are standing in a dark, empty town, because the option with the best violation rate and the lowest cost served nobody. That lands somewhere a paragraph cannot reach.

Worth adding, once the world exists

Things that are only possible because it is a shared, inhabited place.

Multiplayer

Four learners, one station each

No central controller. They have to coordinate by talking to each other. That is the distributed-control problem, and it cannot be staged any other way. The strongest argument for building this as a world rather than an app.

Ethics

The resident at the end of the line

Bus 18's household has worse voltage than bus 2's, permanently, because of where they live. That is energy equity, and in a world you can go and meet them.

Resilience

The storm scenario

A line section fails. Radial means everything downstream goes dark. Now the EV fleet is a resource, not a load — vehicle-to-grid holding up the clinic until the crew arrives.

Virtual laboratory

Build mode

Plan the town yourself: place stations, size a battery, put solar on roofs. Run a year. Get a sustainability report out the other end.

Rendered with a hand-written software rasteriser on a 2D canvas — no 3D library, no external assets, nothing loaded from the network. A production build would use WebGL through react-three-fiber against the same simulation module, which already has no DOM dependency of any kind. This page is a design study, not the submission.
